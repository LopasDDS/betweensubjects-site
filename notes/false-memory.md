Elizabeth Loftus and the implanting of memories.

The early work: Loftus and Palmer (1974). Participants watched a film of a car
accident and were asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed",
"collided", "bumped", "hit" or "contacted" each other. The verb changed the
speed estimates. A week later, those who had heard "smashed" were more likely
to report having seen broken glass in the film. There was no broken glass.

The famous one: Loftus and Pickrell (1995), usually called "Lost in the Mall".
Twenty-four participants were given short accounts of four childhood events,
supplied by a family member. Three were real. The fourth — being lost in a
shopping centre as a young child, and helped by an elderly stranger — was
invented. About a quarter came to report remembering it, some adding details
of their own that nobody had supplied.

Later studies implanted more elaborate events. Wade and colleagues (2002) used
doctored photographs to give people memories of a childhood hot-air balloon
ride that never happened. Others have produced memories of spilling punch at a
wedding, or of being attacked by an animal.

Scoboria and colleagues (2017) pooled eight of these studies and found that
roughly a third of participants showed signs of a false memory, and around a
quarter accepted the event as having happened.

What it establishes: memory is reconstructive rather than a recording. It is
assembled at the moment of recall from fragments, expectation and whatever the
question implies — and confidence in a memory does not track its accuracy.
This work reshaped how courts treat eyewitness testimony.

Limits that must be stated. The 1995 study had twenty-four participants and
its exact rate should be given as "about a quarter" rather than a precise
figure. Brewin and Andrews (2017) argued the rate of full, richly recalled
false memories is lower than the headline numbers suggest, and that many
counted cases are partial — the person accepts the event happened without
actually remembering it. The conditions are also specific: a trusted family
member vouching for the event, and repeated suggestion over several sessions.

What this must not be heard as. It does not show that memory is generally
unreliable, that most memories are invented, or that people who report abuse
are not to be believed. The finding is that memories *can* be created under
particular, engineered conditions — not that they usually are. This research
has been used in court to discredit testimony, Loftus's expert witness work is
genuinely contested, and the topic sits over real allegations by real people.
The script must not imply that someone's account of their own life is
suspect, and must not use "false memory" as a euphemism for lying: the whole
point is that the people in these studies were sincere.

Accurate summary: under the right conditions a person can be given a detailed,
sincerely held memory of something that never happened — which says something
about how memory is built, and much less than it is often used to say about
whether to believe someone.
