Muzafer Sherif, the Robbers Cave experiment, and the study that came first.

The famous one: Sherif and colleagues, Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma,
summer 1954. Twenty-two boys aged eleven and twelve, all white, Protestant,
middle-class, from Oklahoma City, none of whom knew each other beforehand.
They were brought to the camp in two separate buses and kept apart for the
first week, long enough for each group to name itself — the Rattlers and the
Eagles — and make its own flag.

Stage two put the groups against each other in a tournament: baseball, tug of
war, tent-pitching, cabin inspection, with a trophy, medals and pocket knives
for the winners and nothing for the losers. Hostility escalated fast. The
Eagles burned the Rattlers' flag; the Rattlers raided the Eagles' cabin,
overturned beds and took private property. Staff had to physically separate
them.

Stage three removed the hostility again by giving both groups a problem
neither could solve alone. The camp's water supply failed and the boys spent
about three quarters of an hour finding and clearing the blockage together.
A truck carrying food would not start, and they pulled it with the same rope
they had used for tug of war. They pooled money to hire a film. By the end,
most of the boys' friendship choices crossed group lines, and they asked to
go home on one bus rather than two.

What it establishes: competition over something only one group can have is
enough to produce hostility between groups with no prior grievance, and
contact alone does not undo it — a shared goal that needs both sides does.
This is the founding evidence for realistic conflict theory.

The part that is usually left out. Robbers Cave was the third camp study, not
the first, and the one before it failed. In 1953, at Middle Grove in New York
state, Sherif ran essentially the same design and could not make it work. The
boys had been allowed to mix before they were split, and had friendships
across the two groups. When the staff tried to start the friction stage the
boys would not take it up. One of Sherif's assistants pulled down a tent
belonging to one group and trampled their belongings, expecting them to blame
the other group; the boys worked out that the adults were behind it. They
turned on the researchers rather than on each other. Sherif ended the study
and did not publish it. It was reconstructed from his own field notes and
from interviews with surviving participants by Gina Perry, in "The Lost Boys"
(2018).

At Robbers Cave the following summer, the boys were kept from meeting until
each group had already formed. Several of the tournament events — cabin
inspection, tent-pitching — were scored by the staff rather than won
outright, which gave the researchers room to keep the contest close. The
water failure and the stalled truck in stage three were staged by the
experimenters; Sherif's own published account says so.

Limits that must be stated. Twenty-two boys, one camp, one summer, a sample
chosen to be as similar to each other as possible. The 1953 study is known
mainly through Perry's archival work and the participants' memories decades
later, so it is evidence that the design failed once, not a measured result
with a number attached. Sherif's stage-three manipulations were disclosed in
his own writing and are not a hidden scandal — the concealed part is the
earlier failure and the lengths taken to avoid repeating it. Perry's reading
of Sherif's motives is her interpretation, well sourced but contested.

What this must not be heard as. It does not mean the Robbers Cave findings
are fabricated or that realistic conflict theory is wrong; group conflict
over contested resources has been demonstrated many times since, and the
superordinate-goal result has held up better than most findings of its era.
Nor does it mean the boys were actors. What it means is narrower and more
interesting: the experiment needed particular conditions to produce its
effect, and one of those conditions was that the two groups were strangers.

Accurate summary: the most-cited demonstration that competition turns groups
against each other worked on the second attempt. The first attempt failed
because the boys already knew each other, saw what the adults were doing, and
sided with each other against them — and the published design quietly made
sure that could not happen again.
