The lemmings did not jump. They were thrown.

What everyone knows: that lemmings, when their numbers get too high, march to
the sea and hurl themselves off a cliff to their deaths. It is used as a
shorthand for mindless herd behaviour, usually about people.

Where the picture came from. "White Wilderness", a Disney True-Life Adventure
directed by James Algar, released in 1958. It won the Academy Award for Best
Documentary Feature and the Golden Bear at Berlin. Its most famous sequence
shows a mass of lemmings streaming across the ground and pouring over a cliff
into water. It is the reason the image exists in your head at all.

How that sequence was made. The film was shot in Alberta — landlocked, hundreds
of miles from the Arctic Ocean, and not lemming country. The lemmings were
bought from Inuit children in Manitoba and transported there. The "migration"
was a few dozen animals on a turntable, filmed with tight angles and careful
cutting so a small group read as a horde. Then they were driven off a riverbank
above the Bow River near Canmore and filmed falling. The animals did not choose
anything. They were pushed.

Who found out, and when. CBC's current affairs programme The Fifth Estate,
in a documentary called "Cruel Camera" about staged animal footage in film.
Presenter Bob McKeown traced the sequence to the Bow River site. The same
investigation found that a polar bear cub filmed tumbling down an Arctic slope
had been shot in a studio in Calgary.

What lemmings actually do. The population cycles hard — roughly three to five
years between peaks — because they breed fast when food is good and crash when
food runs out and predators arrive. At a peak they disperse: they leave, in
numbers, looking for somewhere less crowded. Dispersal is not migration and
has no destination. They will reach a river or a lake, build up along the
shore, and swim. Some drown. That is an accident of crossing water in numbers,
not an intention, and it is the kernel of truth the film inflated into a
death march.

Limits that must be stated. Sources differ on whether the CBC investigation
aired in 1982 or 1983; the programme and the finding are not in doubt. The film
did not necessarily invent the belief — a wildlife biologist's summary is that
it confused dispersal with migration and embellished something real — but it is
what fixed the image. And the Walt Disney Family Museum's position is that a
freelance wildlife photographer working for the production staged the sequence
without the knowledge or approval of Walt Disney or the studio. Whether that
holds, the film carried it.

What this must not be heard as. Not that nature documentaries are generally
faked, and not that lemming population crashes are made up — the cycles are
real and well documented. The narrower point is that a specific, famous,
award-winning piece of footage showed an animal behaviour that does not exist,
staged by the people filming it, and it took roughly a quarter of a century for
anyone to say so in public. In the meantime the word entered the language.

Accurate summary: the lemming death-march is a scene from a 1958 Disney
documentary that won an Oscar, filmed hundreds of miles from the sea with
animals bought and trucked in, herded on a turntable to look like a horde and
then pushed off a riverbank. Real lemmings do disperse when crowded, and some
drown crossing water. None of them line up to die.
