How much sharper an eagle's eyesight actually is, measured.

The popular claim. Eagles are routinely said to see four to eight times
better than a human, sometimes more. The figure is repeated without a source
attached to it, and the thing being multiplied is rarely specified - sharpness,
distance, and field of view are different quantities.

The study. Lindsay Reymond, "Spatial visual acuity of the eagle Aquila audax:
a behavioural, optical and anatomical investigation", Vision Research, volume
25, issue 10, 1985. The subject was the wedge-tailed eagle, Australia's largest
raptor, and the work combined three independent approaches: a behavioural test,
measurement of the eye's optics, and anatomical counts of the retina.

What the behavioural test did. A small number of trained captive eagles were
taught to discriminate a striped pattern from a plain grey field of the same
average brightness, choosing the striped one for a food reward. The stripes
were then made progressively finer until the bird could no longer tell them
apart. The finest grating it could still reliably pick out gives its acuity in
the same units used to measure human vision.

What it found. The wedge-tailed eagle's acuity came out at roughly twice that
of a human with good eyesight - the paper reports a figure of about 2.4 times
under its conditions. That is a real and substantial advantage, and it is the
highest acuity reliably measured in any animal. It is not four to eight times.

Why the eagle's eye is better at all. The retina's receptor cells are packed
more densely than a human's, and the eagle's eye is large relative to its head,
which produces a bigger image on that retina. Both effects push in the same
direction, and the anatomical counts in the paper agree with what the behaving
bird could do - three methods converging on one answer is the strength of the
study.

Limits that must be stated. This is one species. A small number of captive
trained birds were tested, under laboratory lighting, on a laboratory task.
Grating acuity is one well-defined thing vision does, not the whole of it:
it says nothing about motion detection, ultraviolet sensitivity, depth
judgement, or how well a bird picks a moving rabbit out of scrub from height,
which is the thing people usually mean. An animal can be unremarkable on a
grating chart and formidable in the air.

What this must not be heard as. It is not a claim that eagle vision is
overrated. Twice human acuity is an enormous margin in a biological system,
and a bird that also flies at height sees detail no person could resolve from
the same position. The narrow point is that the specific multiplier in
circulation is larger than anything the measurement supports, and that the
measured figure comes from a real experiment anyone can look up.

Accurate summary: a wedge-tailed eagle trained to pick fine stripes out of a
grey field resolved them at about twice the fineness a person can, roughly
2.4 times by the paper's figure, which is the sharpest vision reliably
measured in any animal and well short of the four-to-eight-times claim that
circulates.
