Clever Hans, the horse that could apparently count.

The claim: a horse in Berlin, owned by a retired schoolteacher named Wilhelm
von Osten, who appeared to do arithmetic. Asked a question, Hans answered by
striking the ground with his hoof — the right number of taps, then a stop. He
was credited with addition, subtraction, telling the time, identifying
colours and spelling out words. The exhibitions were free; von Osten never
charged, which is part of why so many people believed it.

The first investigation got it wrong. In September 1904 the psychologist Carl
Stumpf assembled a panel of thirteen — a veterinarian, a cavalry officer,
schoolteachers, a circus manager, the director of the Berlin zoological
gardens — to look for trickery. They found none, and said so. That verdict
was correct and useless: there was no trick, and the horse still could not
count.

What actually settled it. In 1907 Oskar Pfungst, working in Stumpf's
laboratory, varied the things the earlier panel had left constant. He changed
who asked the question, whether that person knew the answer themselves, how
far away they stood, and whether Hans could see them at all.

The numbers. When Hans could see the questioner he answered correctly on 89
per cent of trials — 50 of 56. When he could not, he was right on 6 per cent
— 2 of 35. The pattern held regardless of who was asking, and it collapsed
whenever the questioner did not know the answer.

The mechanism. As the taps climbed toward the correct number, the person
asking grew very slightly tense — a change of posture, of expression, of
breathing — and released that tension the instant the right number was
reached. Hans had learned to start tapping and to stop on that release. It is
an extraordinary piece of animal perception, just not the one being claimed.

Von Osten was not a fraud, and this matters. He had no idea he was signalling
anything, and he never accepted Pfungst's explanation; he went on exhibiting
Hans until his death in 1909. The cue was invisible to the man producing it.

What it established. The case is the origin of routine double-blind
procedure, and of the observer-expectancy effect — the experimenter's
knowledge of the expected answer leaking into the result through channels
nobody has noticed. An artefact of this kind is still called a Clever Hans
effect, and it is why work on animal cognition and on human subjects is
designed so the person collecting the data does not know what they should be
seeing.

Limits that must be stated. Pfungst never managed to reproduce Hans's errors
reliably by producing the cues deliberately himself, which is a genuine loose
end in an otherwise convincing account. The exact figures above come from
Pfungst's own trials. And this is one horse and one investigation: it
demonstrates that a striking result can have an unnoticed channel behind it,
not that animals cannot learn anything difficult.

What this must not be heard as. It is not that Hans was stupid, or that the
people who believed it were fools. Reading a human being's involuntary
posture accurately enough to stop on the right beat, across different
questioners, is a real and rare ability. The thirteen experts who cleared him
were asking the right question — is there a trick? — and the answer was no.
The failure was in the question, not in their honesty, and that is the part
worth taking seriously: an experiment can be run in good faith by careful
people and still measure the wrong thing.

Accurate summary: a horse that appeared to do arithmetic was reading
involuntary changes in the questioner's body and stopping when they relaxed —
89 per cent right when he could see the person asking, 6 per cent when he
could not — and the investigation that worked this out is why double-blind
procedure is standard.
