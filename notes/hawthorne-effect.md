The Hawthorne effect, and the data that turned up eighty years late.

The studies: at the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric plant in Cicero,
Illinois, a series of experiments ran from 1924. The first set varied the
electric lighting on the factory floor. A later one, the Relay Assembly Test
Room, began in 1927 and followed a small group of women assembling telephone
relays while rest breaks, hours and refreshments were changed around them.
Output was counted mechanically, by the finished relays each worker dropped
down a chute.

The claim that travelled. That productivity rose after every change — brighter
light, dimmer light, more breaks, fewer breaks, and again when the original
conditions came back. From which: it was not the lighting or the breaks, it
was being watched. The effect carries the plant's name and is taught as the
reason a control group is needed at all.

Two things about the name. Nobody at Hawthorne called it that. "Hawthorne
effect" was coined in 1953 by John R. P. French, and given wide currency by
Henry Landsberger's 1958 re-analysis — decades after the work ended. The name
came from the interpretation, not from the experimenters.

The reversal, in 2011. Steven Levitt and John List found the original
illumination data, which had been thought lost, and analysed it properly for
the first time. Their conclusion on the famous pattern is blunt: existing
descriptions of the supposedly remarkable data patterns prove to be entirely
fictional. Output tracked the day of the week and the pay-period cycle far
more closely than it tracked any change in the lighting. The paper is "Was
There Really a Hawthorne Effect at the Hawthorne Plant?", American Economic
Journal: Applied Economics.

And the relay room. Stephen Jones had already gone back to those records in
1992. Once a new incentive pay scheme and an ordinary learning curve were
accounted for, there was very little variation left to attribute to being
observed. The women were being paid differently and getting better at the job.

Limits that must be stated, in both directions. Levitt and List did not find
nothing: they report weaker, more circumscribed evidence consistent with some
reactivity — output somewhat higher while a manipulation was actively running
than in the gaps between manipulations. So something is there; it is just far
smaller and far less dramatic than the story. Reported details differ between
secondary accounts even on the direction of the weekly pattern, which is a
reason to state the shape of the finding rather than the arithmetic.

What this must not be heard as. Not that people behave identically whether or
not they are observed — reactivity and demand characteristics are real, are
well documented elsewhere, and are why blinding exists. Not that the Hawthorne
researchers faked anything. What collapsed is narrower and more embarrassing:
the single most cited demonstration of the effect does not demonstrate it. A
conclusion was written up, repeated, simplified, named, and taught for eighty
years while the numbers underneath it sat unexamined.

Accurate summary: the study that taught everyone people work harder when
watched was, for eighty years, described by people who had not seen its data.
When two economists finally found the original records, the famous pattern —
output rising after every change, even the ones that made the room darker —
was not in them. Something small survives. The story does not.
