Solomon Asch's conformity experiments.

Run at Swarthmore College and published from 1951, most fully in Psychological
Monographs in 1956.

The setup: a participant sits with a group and is shown a standard line and
three comparison lines. The task is trivial — which comparison line matches
the standard. The answer is not ambiguous. In a control condition with nobody
else present, people were right well over 99 per cent of the time.

Everyone else in the room is working for Asch. On certain trials they all give
the same wrong answer, out loud, before the real participant answers.

The version everybody repeats is that people conform to a group even when the
group is obviously wrong, and it is usually attached to a figure of about 75
per cent. That figure is real, and it is being used to say something the study
did not find.

What the data actually show.

Seventy-five per cent is the share of participants who went along with the
group at least once across the whole run. It is not the share of answers that
conformed. Counted by response, roughly a third of answers on the critical
trials followed the group, which means about two thirds did not. The most
common thing a participant did was give the right answer while the room gave
the wrong one.

About a quarter of participants never conformed on a single trial.

The finding Asch thought mattered most is the one that almost never gets
repeated. When he broke the unanimity — one confederate, giving the correct
answer, against the rest — conformity collapsed to a fraction of its previous
level, in the region of a quarter of it. The exact figures vary between
conditions and should be given as an order of magnitude rather than a precise
number. It was not the size of the majority that mattered so much as whether
it was unanimous: a single visible ally restored independence.

Asch wrote about being struck by the independence people showed, not by the
conformity. The popular telling reverses his emphasis.

Limits worth stating. The original participants were all male college
students, and the samples were small. Bond and Smith's meta-analysis (1996)
pooled 133 studies across 17 countries and found the effect is real but varies
by culture, and that measured conformity in United States samples declined
across the decades after Asch ran it. It is not a fixed constant of human
nature.

Accurate summary: the study is usually produced as proof that people are
sheep. Its own numbers show most answers were independent, a quarter of people
never once went along, and one other person telling the truth was enough to
bring the rest back.
