Betty the crow, the wire hook, and the field study that took the invention
back fourteen years later.

The famous moment: in 2002 a New Caledonian crow named Betty, in Alex
Kacelnik's lab at Oxford, was filmed taking a straight piece of garden wire,
wedging one end, bending it into a hook, and using it to lift a small bucket
of food out of a vertical tube. It was published by Weir, Chappell and
Kacelnik in Science that year, in a paper of barely a page. The trial was not
even the planned experiment — her companion Abel had made off with the
ready-made hook the researchers had supplied, leaving Betty with only the
straight wire.

Why it mattered so much. Wire is not a material any crow encounters in New
Caledonia. Betty appeared to size up a novel problem, work out that a hook
would solve it, and manufacture one from a substance she could have no
evolved routine for — without trial and error, and repeatedly, bending the
wire on nine of ten later trials. For a while it was the strongest single
piece of evidence that a non-human animal could invent a tool by understanding
what the job required. It went into textbooks and documentaries as exactly
that.

The reversal, in 2016. Christian Rutz and colleagues at St Andrews ran field
experiments with eighteen wild-caught New Caledonian crows in an aviary, which
made eighty-five hooked stick tools to winkle food out of drilled logs. Ten of
the eighteen birds bent their tools while making them — deliberately putting
curvature into the working end. Tool bending was not a flash of insight. It
was part of what this species already does, in the wild, as a routine step in
ordinary tool manufacture. The paper was "Tool bending in New Caledonian
crows," in Royal Society Open Science.

Why that lands on Betty specifically. Betty was wild-caught, not captive-bred.
She had lived as a New Caledonian crow before she ever reached Oxford, which
means she could have brought the bending action with her. The 2016 authors put
it plainly: she may have been expressing behaviour that was already part of her
natural repertoire rather than solving the problem afresh, and her performance
can potentially be explained without invoking exceptional cognitive processes.
The novelty everyone reacted to was the wire. The motor routine was not novel
at all.

Limits that must be stated, in both directions. The 2016 team say outright
that their observations do not rule out that Betty understood what she was
doing — showing a behaviour exists in the repertoire is not the same as showing
it was applied mindlessly. Nobody caught anyone out: the original authors could
not have known about wild tool-bending in 2002, because the field work had not
been done. And bending a twig while making a stick tool is not identical to
bending wire around a novel obstacle; how far the technique transfers has not
been fully settled. This is a live disagreement, not a closed case.

What this must not be heard as. Not that Betty was stupid, not that the 2002
paper was wrong or careless, and not that crows are unintelligent — New
Caledonian crows are genuinely remarkable tool users and that is not in
dispute. The claim that thinned is narrower: that this particular act was
invention from scratch. What changed was not the bird's behaviour but what was
known about the background it came from.

Accurate summary: a crow bending a wire into a hook was for years the best
evidence an animal could invent a tool on the spot — until researchers went to
New Caledonia, watched wild crows make their own tools, and found that ten of
eighteen of them bend the shaft as a matter of course. Betty was wild-caught.
She may have arrived already knowing the move, and the only genuinely new
thing in the room was the wire.
