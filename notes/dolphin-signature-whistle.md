Wild dolphins were played copies of their own whistles with the voice stripped
out, and called back.

The background. Bottlenose dolphins each develop a signature whistle: a
distinctive melodic contour, invented early in life and kept for decades. Other
dolphins occasionally copy a particular animal's contour. The obvious guess is
that the copy works like a name - a way of addressing that individual - but a
guess is all it is until the animal being addressed does something about it.

The crucial control. A whistle carries two kinds of information: the tune, and
the voice producing it. If a dolphin responds to a copy of its own whistle, it
might be reacting to how it sounds rather than to the pattern. To separate
those, the researchers used computer-generated whistles with the voice
characteristics removed, leaving only the contour. Anything the dolphin did in
response had to be a response to the shape of the tune.

The study. Stephanie King and Vincent Janik, "Bottlenose dolphins can use
learned vocal labels to address each other", Proceedings of the National
Academy of Sciences, volume 110, 2013. The playbacks were done on free-ranging
dolphins off the east coast of Scotland, in the Moray Firth and St Andrews Bay.

The design. Three conditions. A synthetic copy of the focal animal's own
signature whistle (12 playbacks). A synthetic whistle belonging to a familiar
animal from the same population (12). And a synthetic whistle from an
unfamiliar animal in a different population entirely, modelled on captive
dolphins (10).

What they found. Hearing its own whistle, the group answered by producing that
same whistle back in 8 of the 12 trials. That differed from the familiar
control (Barnard's exact test, P = 0.04) and from the unfamiliar control
(P = 0.001). Responses were scored by five observers who did not know which
condition they were rating, with moderate agreement between them.

Limits that must be stated. These are small numbers: 12, 12 and 10 playbacks.
The researchers could not tell which animal in the group replied, so the
response is properly credited to the group rather than to the individual whose
whistle was played - which is a real gap in a claim about addressing an
individual. Group sizes averaged around 13 animals. A post hoc check failed to
confirm four of the synthetic stimuli as signature whistles. And copying is
rare in wild dolphins, so the behaviour being studied is uncommon to begin with.

What this must not be heard as. "Name" is an analogy and the authors use the
more careful phrase, learned vocal label. Nothing here shows a dolphin
understands a whistle as standing for a creature, or uses it to talk about an
absent one. What was demonstrated is narrower and still unusual: an animal
responding specifically to a learned sound pattern associated with itself,
after the voice cues that might have given the game away were deliberately
removed.

Accurate summary: wild bottlenose dolphins off Scotland were played synthetic
copies of their own signature whistles, stripped of voice characteristics so
only the contour remained, and the group called that whistle back in 8 of 12
trials - significantly more than for whistles belonging to familiar or
unfamiliar animals.
