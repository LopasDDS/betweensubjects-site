Sheep were trained to pick a familiar face out of a pair of photographs, and
the result came with a published argument attached.

The study. Franziska Knolle, Rita P. Goncalves and A. Jennifer Morton, "Sheep
recognize familiar and unfamiliar human faces from two-dimensional images",
Royal Society Open Science, volume 4, issue 11, 2017.

What they did. Eight sheep were trained at a computer screen showing two
images side by side. Choosing the correct one earned a food reward. The faces
used in training were four celebrities, learned from photographic portraits -
chosen simply because they were faces the sheep could have no prior contact
with, not for any quality of fame.

The first result. Once trained, the sheep picked the learned face out of a
pair well above chance. The researchers then tilted the portraits, showing the
same person from a different angle the sheep had never seen. Performance fell,
from about 79 per cent correct to about 67 per cent - but stayed well above
chance. The size of that drop is similar to what happens when people are given
the same kind of task, which is the detail that makes the result interesting
rather than merely cute.

The second result. The sheep were then shown photographs of their own
handler, someone they knew in person but had never seen as an image, against
an unfamiliar face. They picked the handler in about 72 per cent of trials,
with no training for that pairing at all.

The published objection. This did not go unchallenged. Towler and colleagues
published a response in the same journal in 2019 arguing the conclusions were
preliminary: too few animals, too few face stimuli, and no direct side-by-side
comparison between sheep and human performance, which the claim of
human-comparable ability leans on. A further objection is that the sheep were
never tested on sheep faces - people are far better with human faces than with
sheep faces, and a sheep would presumably be better with its own species, so a
test using only human faces measures something narrower than "face
recognition" in general. Keith Kendrick replied defending the work as
consistent with thirty years of prior behavioural and neural evidence. The
original authors did not concede an error.

Limits that must be stated. Eight animals, four training faces, one handler.
The tilted-image comparison with human performance is a comparison with
results from other studies rather than humans tested alongside these sheep on
this apparatus. Trained discrimination at a screen for a food reward is a
narrow and well-defined skill, and calling it recognition imports more than
the trials measured.

What this must not be heard as. It is not evidence that a sheep knows who you
are in the way you would mean it, nor that sheep think about faces the way
people do. And the dispute above is not a scandal or a debunking: a short
paper with eight animals drawing a comparison to human ability is exactly the
sort of claim that should attract a written objection, and the objection
appearing in the same journal is the process working, not failing.

Accurate summary: eight sheep learned to pick particular human faces out of
pairs of photographs, still managed it above chance when the faces were tilted
to an unseen angle - dropping from roughly 79 to 67 per cent, a fall similar
in size to the one people show - and chose their own handler's photograph in
about 72 per cent of trials without being trained to. A published response
argued the sample and stimuli were too small to support the comparison with
human ability.
