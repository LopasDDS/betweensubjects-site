The decoy effect, formally the asymmetric dominance effect.

Demonstrated by Huber, Payne & Puto (1982), "Adding Asymmetrically Dominated
Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis", in the
Journal of Consumer Research.

The mechanism: adding a third option that is clearly worse than one existing
option in every respect, but not worse than the other, shifts choices toward
the option that dominates it. The decoy exists to be rejected, not chosen. Its
presence changes the share of choices between the two original options, which
standard rational-choice models say should not happen.

Dan Ariely popularised it in Predictably Irrational using a magazine
subscription price list of three tiers: web only, print only, and print plus
web, where print only and print plus web carried the same price. That made
print only strictly dominated, and it was the decoy.

In Ariely's classroom demonstration with 100 students, 84 chose print plus web,
16 chose web only, and none chose print only. With the dominated option
removed, 68 chose web only and 32 chose print plus web. These figures come from
Ariely's own demonstration rather than a controlled replication, and should be
described that way.

Later research finds the effect is real but context-sensitive. It is weaker or
absent for some product categories and presentation formats.

Note on illustrations: three cinema popcorn sizes, or three coffee cups, are a
common way to picture the effect. They are illustrations, not studies. No
finding may be attributed to them.

Accurate summary: adding a third option that nobody picks can change which of
the other two people do pick, which standard models of rational choice say
should not happen. The effect is real and well replicated in the laboratory,
but it is context-sensitive rather than a universal lever, and the famous
magazine-subscription figures come from a classroom demonstration rather than
a controlled study.
