Defaults, and the organ donation figures.

Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein, "Do Defaults Save Lives?", Science, 2003.

What they compared. European countries fall into two groups. In opt-in
countries you are not a donor unless you register as one. In opt-out, or
presumed consent, countries you are a donor unless you register that you are
not. The forms differ; the question does not.

The gap is enormous. In their figures, effective consent in opt-out countries
sat in the high nineties — Austria at 99.98 per cent — while opt-in countries
were in the teens or lower, with Germany around 12 per cent and Denmark
around 4. Austria and Germany are the pair worth naming: neighbours, closely
comparable in language, wealth and medical systems, separated by which box the
form leaves ticked.

They also ran a controlled online experiment, randomly assigning people to an
opt-in, opt-out or neutral form. Consent was roughly twice as high when the
default was to be a donor. That matters, because it rules out the objection
that these countries simply differ in how people feel about donation.

Why defaults work. Changing one takes effort. It also reads as a
recommendation — the default looks like what a sensible person does, or what
the institution expects. And whichever option you start from tends to feel
like the one you own.

The part that gets left out. Consent on paper is not an organ in an operating
theatre. The difference in *actual* donation rates between the two systems is
real but far smaller than the consent gap suggests — Abadie and Gay (Journal
of Health Economics, 2006) estimated presumed consent countries run roughly a
quarter to a third higher, once other factors are controlled for. Not 99
against 12. Families are still consulted in practice, and a registered donor
whose family refuses is usually not a donor.

Spain is the country most often produced as proof that presumed consent works,
and it is the weakest example available. Spain has had a presumed consent law
since 1979, and its donation rate was unremarkable for the decade that
followed. What changed was the creation of the national transplant
organisation in 1989 — full-time donor coordinators in hospitals, intensive
care staff trained to identify potential donors, and families approached by
people who do that work for a living. The law was already there. The
infrastructure was the variable.

Accurate summary: the default is one of the strongest levers in behavioural
science and it moves consent by enormous margins. It moves actual donations
far less, and the country held up as the proof is really evidence for staffing
and process.
