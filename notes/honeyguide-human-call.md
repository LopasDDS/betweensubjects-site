A wild bird that answers a human call, and the experiment that showed it was
the call and not the human.

The partnership. In parts of Africa, greater honeyguides lead people to wild
bees' nests. The bird finds the nest; the person makes the fire, takes the
stings and opens it; the bird eats the beeswax comb left behind, which it can
digest and which almost nothing else will touch. Neither side could get what
it wants as cheaply alone. The birds are wild. Nobody raises them, trains them
or rewards them on a schedule.

The call. Yao honey-hunters in northern Mozambique summon honeyguides with a
specific sound passed down the generations: a loud trill followed by a short
grunt, written "brrr-hm". The obvious sceptical reading is that the bird is
simply responding to a human being nearby, and that any noise would do.

The study. Claire Spottiswoode, Keith Begg and Colleen Begg, "Reciprocal
signaling in honeyguide-human mutualism", Science, volume 353, 2016. The work
was done in the Niassa National Reserve with Yao honey-hunters on real
honey-hunting trips.

What they did. Rather than argue about it, they ran playback trials. Walking
the same routes, they played one of three recordings: the "brrr-hm" call; the
same honey-hunters' voices saying arbitrary words; and the call of a different
bird species. If the bird were responding merely to human presence, all three
should work about equally.

What they found. With the "brrr-hm" call, the chance of being guided by a
honeyguide rose from 33 per cent to 66 per cent. The chance of actually being
shown a bees' nest rose from 16 per cent to 54 per cent. The call roughly
tripled the odds of the whole interaction succeeding. The birds were
discriminating between human sounds, not simply noticing a human.

Limits that must be stated. This is one population, in one reserve, responding
to one culture's call. It does not establish that the same sound works
elsewhere, and honey-hunting peoples in other regions use different calls. The
trials happened on working hunts in open country rather than under laboratory
control. And the study measured what the birds did, not how they came to do
it: whether a young honeyguide learns the local call or arrives with some
predisposition is a separate question this experiment was not built to answer.

What this must not be heard as. It is not language, and the bird is not
understanding a word. What was demonstrated is that one specific human sound
reliably gets a different response from other human sounds - which is a real
and unusual thing for a wild animal, and quite enough on its own. A related
piece of folklore, that honeyguides also lead honey badgers to nests, is much
weaker: it is widely repeated and has very little evidence behind it, and
should not be carried along on the strength of this result.

Accurate summary: greater honeyguides in northern Mozambique were played three
sounds on real honey-hunting trips, and the traditional Yao "brrr-hm" call
doubled the chance of being guided, from 33 to 66 per cent, and raised the
chance of being shown a bees' nest from 16 to 54 per cent, against arbitrary
human words and another bird's call - a wild, untrained animal responding to a
specific signal from people.
