Detection dogs, and the handler at the other end of the lead.

The study: Lit, Schweitzer and Oberbauer, "Handler beliefs affect scent
detection dog outcomes", Animal Cognition, 2011. Eighteen certified drug and
explosive detection dog and handler teams each ran a series of short search
scenarios in a set of rooms.

The design. Nothing was hidden anywhere. No condition contained any drug or
explosive scent at all, so every alert the dogs gave was wrong by definition.
What varied was what the handlers were told and what was left in the rooms.
In two conditions the handlers were told a piece of paper marked where the
scent was — a lie, there was no scent. In two others a decoy was left out,
food or a toy, to interest the dog rather than the handler.

The result. Across the runs the teams alerted more than two hundred times in
rooms that contained nothing. Alerts clustered where the handler had been told
to expect one. The handler's belief moved the outcome more than the decoys
did: the dogs were, in the end, reading their people.

Why this is the same story as a horse in Berlin. Oskar Pfungst showed in 1907
that Clever Hans was answering the questioner's involuntary posture rather
than the arithmetic, and nobody involved knew it was happening. A hundred and
four years later, the same effect turns up in a trained working animal whose
alerts are used as grounds to search people and their cars. The handlers in
this study were not cheating. Like von Osten, they did not know they were
signalling.

Limits that must be stated. Eighteen teams, one study, and scenarios staged
in rooms rather than recorded on real deployments. It does not measure how
often detection dogs are wrong in the field, and it does not show that they
cannot detect anything — the point is narrower: when the handler expects a
find, the team produces one. Later work has continued on how much a handler's
knowledge of the task changes a dog's search behaviour, so this is an active
area rather than a single result standing alone.

What this must not be heard as. Not that detection dogs are useless, not that
handlers are dishonest, and not that any particular alert was false. It is
also not a claim about the law, which is a separate argument this study does
not settle. The finding is about procedure: an alert is produced by a dog and
a person together, and where that matters, the person has to be kept from
knowing what they are supposed to find. That is the same fix Pfungst's work
produced in 1907, and the reason double-blind procedure exists at all.

Accurate summary: eighteen trained detection dog teams searched rooms that
contained no drugs and no explosives, and alerted over two hundred times —
most often exactly where their handlers had been told, falsely, that
something was hidden. The dogs were reading the humans, which is Clever Hans
with a working animal and higher stakes.
