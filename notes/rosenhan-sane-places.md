Rosenhan's "On Being Sane in Insane Places".

Published by David Rosenhan in Science in 1973, and one of the most influential
papers in the history of psychiatry.

What the paper claimed. Eight healthy people, Rosenhan among them, presented at
twelve psychiatric hospitals saying they heard a voice saying "empty",
"hollow" and "thud". All but one were admitted, mostly with a diagnosis of
schizophrenia. Once inside they behaved entirely normally, yet no member of
staff identified them as well. They stayed an average of nineteen days, and
were discharged with schizophrenia "in remission" rather than as healthy. A
second part reported that a hospital, told to expect fakes, flagged 41 of 193
incoming patients as suspected impostors, when Rosenhan had sent none.

Its influence was enormous. It is widely credited as a force behind the
overhaul of psychiatric diagnosis that produced DSM-III in 1980, and it fed the
movement away from institutional care.

The corrections. Susannah Cahalan spent years trying to verify the study for
The Great Pretender (2019), and could confirm very little of it.

Rosenhan's own medical record from the hospital he entered does not match what
he published. He told the admitting doctor that the voices were distressing
enough that he had put copper over his ears to block them, and gave indications
of being a danger to himself. On that account, admitting him was a reasonable
clinical decision rather than a diagnostic failure. The paper describes a much
blander presentation.

Cahalan could identify only two of the other pseudopatients with confidence.
One of them, Harry Lando, had his data left out of the paper — his experience
of the hospital had been positive, which did not fit the argument. For several
of the remaining participants she found no evidence that they existed.

There is no documented protocol for the follow-up study with the 41 suspected
impostors.

Rosenhan took an advance for a book about the study and never delivered it.

What should not be overstated. The concerns the paper raised about the
reliability of psychiatric diagnosis were real, and were already being argued
inside psychiatry. Robert Spitzer published a detailed methodological attack in
1975, much of which stands, and he was one of the architects of the reform the
study is credited with. The reform was arguably needed whether or not the study
was sound. Rosenhan died in 2012 and cannot answer Cahalan's findings, which
should be said.

Accurate summary: a study that helped rewrite psychiatric diagnosis appears to
rest on evidence that largely cannot be found, including a case record that
contradicts its own author — and the change it triggered was probably right
anyway.
