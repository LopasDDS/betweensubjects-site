Pigs were shown food that was only visible in a mirror, and most of them went
straight round the barrier to get it. A later attempt to repeat the experiment
largely failed.

What this is not. Not a mark test. Nobody painted a pig and watched for it to
touch the mark. The question here is narrower and in some ways cleaner: can
the animal treat a reflection as information about where things are?

The study. Donald M. Broom, Hilana Sena and Kiera L. Moynihan, "Pigs learn
what a mirror image represents and use it to obtain information", Animal
Behaviour, volume 78, 2009, pages 1037-1041.

What they did. Eight pigs spent about five hours with a mirror in their pen.
Each was then put in a space where a familiar food bowl sat hidden behind a
solid barrier - out of sight, but reflected in the mirror. To reach it, the
animal had to turn away from the image and go round.

What happened. Seven of the eight found the bowl, taking on average about 23
seconds. Pigs that had never seen a mirror before did something different:
they went and looked behind it.

The controls. Three alternatives were checked and ruled out. The pigs were not
tracking the food by smell. They had no standing preference for that corner of
the enclosure. And they did not head for that corner when the bowl was placed
somewhere visible instead.

The replication, which is the part that matters. Gieling and colleagues ran
the experiment again with two groups of eleven piglets and published the
result under the title "Lack of mirror use by pigs to locate food". Only two
pigs in the first group located the hidden bowl. The original finding did not
come back.

How to hold both results. A failed replication does not delete the first
study, and the two are not identical - different animals, ages, housing and
exposure. But it does mean the honest description of the evidence is "one
study found this and a larger attempt did not", rather than "pigs can use
mirrors". The original authors drew a fairly large conclusion from eight
animals, calling it evidence of assessment awareness; that phrase is doing
more work than seven pigs can support even if the result had replicated.

Limits that must be stated. Eight animals in the original, five hours of
mirror exposure, one task. The replication is larger but also not decisive on
its own - a failure to find an effect is not proof there is none, particularly
when the training and setup differ.

What this must not be heard as. Not "pigs are smarter than you think", and not
"the pig study was debunked". The useful thing here is the shape: a striking
small result, a bigger attempt that did not reproduce it, and no tidy ending.
Most findings that reach the public never get the second half reported.

Accurate summary: seven of eight pigs given five hours with a mirror used the
reflection to find food hidden behind a barrier, averaging 23 seconds, with
smell and side-preference ruled out - but a later study with twenty-two
piglets, published as "Lack of mirror use by pigs to locate food", found only
two animals doing it.
