The cat parasite that rewires rats, and the part about you that keeps not
showing up.

The finding everyone repeats. Toxoplasma gondii can only complete its life
cycle inside a cat. In 2000, Berdoy, Webster and Macdonald published "Fatal
attraction in rats infected with Toxoplasma gondii" in Proceedings of the Royal
Society B: rats have an innate aversion to the smell of cat, and infected rats
lost it - in some cases the aversion turned into attraction. A parasite
appearing to steer its host into the mouth of its next host is about as good as
biology gets as a story.

It got better, and then worse. In 2007 a PNAS paper reported that the effect
was highly specific: the behavioural change was aimed at aversion to cat odour
and left other fear responses intact. That specificity is what made it a
hijacking rather than a side effect, and it is the version that spread.

The correction, 2020. Boillat and colleagues, "Neuroinflammation-Associated
Aspecific Manipulation of Mouse Predator Fear by Toxoplasma gondii", Cell
Reports. Infected mice showed lowered general anxiety and more exploratory
behaviour, and their predator aversion was altered with no selectivity toward
cats at all. The size of the behavioural change tracked cyst load - that is,
the amount of inflammation in the brain. Their own framing is that this refutes
the idea of a selective loss of cat fear and points to broad immune-related
changes instead.

So the behaviour is real. The elegance is not. A parasite that makes a rodent
generally braver and less anxious will incidentally get more of its hosts
eaten, which is a duller mechanism than a targeted hijack and does not require
the parasite to know what a cat is.

The human claim. The popular version says latent toxoplasma makes people
reckless, raises the odds of schizophrenia, causes car crashes, and explains the
"crazy cat lady". The strongest test of it is Sugden and colleagues, PLOS ONE,
2016, using the Dunedin birth cohort - 837 people followed since birth, tested
for antibodies. They found no significant association with schizophrenia, and
little evidence of any link to depression, suicidal behaviour, criminality,
personality aberration or neurocognitive impairment. Essentially nothing,
across the board.

Limits that must be stated, and they cut both ways. One cohort of 837 is not
the last word, and other work does report associations - a meta-analysis in
Psychological Medicine found links with suicide attempts and traffic accidents.
So "toxoplasma does nothing to humans" is not established either. What is fair
to say is that the confident popular claim runs well ahead of both bodies of
evidence, and that the best-designed prospective study found nothing where it
should have been easiest to find.

What this must not be heard as. Not that toxoplasma is harmless - it is a
serious risk in pregnancy and for immunocompromised people, and that is not in
dispute. Not that owning a cat is the main way people get it; undercooked meat
and soil matter more. And not that the rat work was wrong: infected rodents do
behave differently, which is the part that survived.

Accurate summary: a parasite really does change infected rodents' behaviour in
a way that helps it reach a cat - but the famous part, that it selectively
deletes the fear of cats, did not survive the follow-up. It makes them
generally less anxious, which is messier and less clever. And the version about
humans, the one that made it into every headline, does not show up in the best
study anyone has run.
