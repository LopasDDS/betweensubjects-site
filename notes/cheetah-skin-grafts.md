Skin was grafted between unrelated wild cheetahs, and none of it was rejected.

Why that is strange. An immune system treats tissue from another individual as
foreign and destroys it. That is why transplants need matched donors and
lifelong drugs. The matching is done on the major histocompatibility complex,
a set of genes so variable that two unrelated individuals almost never carry
the same version. Humans reject an unmatched skin graft in about ten days.
Domestic cats reject one in roughly seven to thirteen.

The study. Stephen J. O'Brien and colleagues, "Genetic basis for species
vulnerability in the cheetah", Science, volume 227, 1985, pages 1428-1434.
Earlier work had already found cheetahs to be remarkably uniform genetically.
This paper surveyed more than 200 structural loci and added a direct test of
the immune system.

What they did. Fourteen reciprocal skin grafts between unrelated cheetahs -
six in South Africa, eight in Oregon. Reciprocal means each animal both gave
and received.

The control that makes it an experiment. A graft that is not rejected could
mean the animals are genetically identical at the relevant genes, or it could
mean the immune systems were not working. To separate those, skin from a
domestic cat was grafted onto cheetah graft beds as well. The cheetahs rejected
the cat skin normally. Their immune systems were intact; they simply did not
see another cheetah's tissue as foreign.

The result. All fourteen cheetah-to-cheetah grafts were accepted. Unrelated
wild animals were, immunologically, as good as identical twins.

Why it mattered at the time. The authors tied this to a set of problems in the
species: difficulty breeding in captivity, high juvenile mortality in captivity
and in the wild, and a high proportion of malformed sperm. A 1983 outbreak of
feline infectious peritonitis had torn through an Oregon breeding colony, and
they suggested the uniformity and the apparent vulnerability to disease might
be connected - a population with no genetic variation has no individuals that
happen to resist.

What has changed since. The headline result stands: cheetahs really do have
extraordinarily low diversity, from a population bottleneck thousands of years
ago. But the sharpest version of the conclusion has been softened. Later work
using finer methods has found more variation at the major histocompatibility
complex than the techniques of 1985 could detect, so "monomorphic" overstates
it. The read of the grafts as proof of total MHC uniformity should be taken as
a result of its period.

Limits that must be stated. Fourteen grafts across two facilities is a small
test by modern standards, and the animals were captive. The link drawn between
low diversity and the breeding and disease problems was an association the
authors proposed, not something the experiment demonstrated.

What this must not be heard as. It does not mean the cheetah is doomed, and
the species' main problems today are habitat loss and conflict with people,
not genetics. Nor is it a story about bad science: a 1985 result refined by
better tools forty years later is the ordinary way this works.

Accurate summary: fourteen reciprocal skin grafts between unrelated cheetahs
were all accepted, while domestic cat skin grafted onto the same animals was
rejected normally - showing the immune systems worked and the cheetahs were
simply too genetically alike to tell each other's tissue apart, though later
methods found more diversity than the 1985 techniques could see.
