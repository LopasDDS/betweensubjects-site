Power posing, and the author who stopped believing it.

The original: Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap, "Power Posing: Brief
Nonverbal Displays Affect Neuroendocrine Levels and Risk Tolerance"
(Psychological Science, 2010). Forty-two participants held either expansive
"high-power" postures or contracted "low-power" ones for two minutes. The paper
reported that the high-power group showed raised testosterone, lowered
cortisol, more risk-taking, and reported feeling more powerful.

Amy Cuddy's 2012 TED talk about it became one of the most watched ever, tens
of millions of views, and the advice — stand like this before an interview —
spread everywhere.

What happened next.

Ranehill and colleagues (Psychological Science, 2015) ran a much larger
replication, around two hundred participants against the original forty-two.
The *feeling* of power replicated. The hormonal changes and the risk-taking
did not.

Simmons and Simonsohn (2017) applied a p-curve analysis across the published
power-posing literature and concluded it contained no evidential value for the
behavioural and physiological effects.

Then the part that makes this unusual. In 2016 Dana Carney, the paper's first
author, published a statement on her own university web page saying plainly
that she does not believe the effects are real. She listed the methodological
problems as she now saw them, said she no longer studies the topic, and
discouraged others from building on it. Authors rarely do this in public, and
almost never about their own most cited work.

The dispute that remains. Amy Cuddy has not accepted that the whole thing
falls. Later work with Cuddy as an author (2018) argued that the self-reported
feeling of power does hold up across many studies, which the 2015 replication
also found. So "you feel more confident" is on far better footing than "your
testosterone rises."

What may not be said. That posture does nothing, or that Cuddy acted in bad
faith. The felt effect survives; the hormonal mechanism is what collapsed. The
episode became unusually personal and public, and describing it as one
researcher being proved a fraud would be both wrong and unfair.

Accurate summary: the most watched psychology talk in the world rests on a
paper whose first author has since written, in public, that she does not
believe the result — while the milder version of the claim, that it changes
how you feel, is still standing.
