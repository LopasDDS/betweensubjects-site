Naked mole-rats do not react to acid or to chilli, and the usual summary -
that they feel no pain - is wrong in a way worth being precise about.

The claim as it circulates. "Naked mole-rats can't feel pain." They can. The
paper's own title says selective, and the selectivity is the finding.

The study. Thomas J. Park and colleagues, including Gary R. Lewin, "Selective
inflammatory pain insensitivity in the African naked mole-rat (Heterocephalus
glaber)", PLoS Biology, volume 6, number 1, 2008, article e13.

What they do not respond to. Two substances that reliably produce pain
behaviour in other mammals produce almost none in naked mole-rats: acid, and
capsaicin, the compound that makes chilli burn.

What they do respond to. They show ordinary pain behaviour in the formalin
test, and after inflammation they become more sensitive to mechanical
stimulation, the same as other animals. What inflammation does not produce in
them is heat hypersensitivity. So the system is not switched off - parts of it
are missing and the rest works.

The strange part, for capsaicin. The receptor capsaicin acts on, TRPV1, is
present in their sensory neurons, and those neurons fire vigorously when
capsaicin is applied. The signal is generated. It just does not turn into pain
behaviour. The explanation offered is that their skin sensory fibres lack
substance P, one of the neuropeptides that carries the message onward. The
wiring is there up to a point and then stops.

The acid case is different. Here the nociceptors themselves do not respond -
acid never generates the signal in the first place. Consistent with that,
supplying the missing neuropeptide does not restore an acid response, because
there is nothing to carry.

Why it might have happened. Naked mole-rats live in large colonies in sealed
burrows where carbon dioxide builds up, and high CO2 makes tissue acidic. An
animal whose nociceptors fire at acid would be in constant discomfort
underground. Acid sensing is widespread in vertebrates - birds, amphibians,
fish - so the likeliest story is that this lineage lost a mechanism that had
become a liability, rather than never having had it.

Limits that must be stated. This is a laboratory study of one species, and the
CO2 explanation is a plausible account of why the trait might be favoured, not
something the experiments tested. "Pain behaviour" means measurable responses -
licking, flinching, withdrawal - and the absence of those is not proof of the
absence of an experience, which is not measurable in a mole-rat.

What this must not be heard as. Not that the animal is invulnerable, or that
it has nothing to teach about human pain beyond curiosity. And not the version
in circulation: it is insensitive to specific stimuli by specific mechanisms,
not immune to pain.

Accurate summary: naked mole-rats show almost no pain behaviour to acid or
capsaicin, but respond normally to other noxious stimuli and develop mechanical
hypersensitivity after inflammation. For capsaicin the receptor is present and
the nerves fire, and the signal fails later for want of substance P; for acid
the nerves never fire at all - plausibly because their burrows are
carbon-dioxide rich enough to make acid sensing a constant nuisance.
