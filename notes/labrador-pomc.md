The Labrador that cannot feel full, and why the assistance dogs carry it most.

The work: Eleanor Raffan and colleagues, "A Deletion in the Canine POMC Gene Is
Associated with Weight and Appetite in Obesity-Prone Labrador Retriever Dogs",
Cell Metabolism, May 2016. They looked for a genetic reason Labradors are the
breed most reliably described as food-obsessed.

What they found. A 14 base-pair deletion in POMC, the gene for
pro-opiomelanocortin. The deletion disrupts the sequences coding for two
peptides, beta-MSH and beta-endorphin, which are part of how the signal to stop
eating gets made after a meal. A dog carrying it is not being badly behaved. It
is working with a damaged off-switch.

The numbers, which are modest and should be stated as such. 310 Labradors were
genotyped for the association, 383 more for how common it is, and 296
flat-coat retrievers as a comparison. Each copy of the deletion was worth about
1.9 kg of body weight and roughly a 10 per cent increase in food motivation on
a standard owner questionnaire. That is a real effect and a small one — a third
of a standard deviation. It is not a switch that makes a dog fat.

The part that turns the story around. In pet Labradors the deletion allele runs
at about 12 per cent. In Labradors selected to become assistance dogs it runs
at 45 per cent — 76 per cent of them carry at least one copy, against 22 per
cent of pets. Nobody was breeding for obesity. They were selecting for
trainability, and a dog that will work hard for food is easier to train. The
trait that makes a Labrador good at guiding a person is the same trait that
makes it fat in an ordinary home.

And the assistance dogs are not fat. They carry the appetite gene at nearly
four times the rate and stay a healthy weight, because their food and exercise
are controlled by someone following a routine. That is the whole finding in one
comparison: the gene sets the appetite, the household sets the weight.

Limits that must be stated. One breed and its close relative — the deletion was
absent from 38 other breeds and found only in the flat-coat retriever, so this
is not an explanation of dog obesity in general, let alone human obesity. The
authors are explicit that owners control how much food and exercise a dog gets,
and they did not claim to quantify how much of obesity the deletion explains.
An association in a few hundred dogs is a starting point, not a mechanism
proven end to end.

What this must not be heard as. Not that a fat Labrador is nobody's
responsibility — the assistance dogs prove the opposite, since the same
genotype stays lean under a controlled routine. Not that a lean Labrador lacks
the gene. And not a licence to test your dog and give up: the practical lesson
runs the other way, because knowing an animal is hungrier than it looks is a
reason to measure the food, not a reason to stop.

Accurate summary: Labradors really do have a genetic reason to be hungry — a
deletion in POMC that damages part of the signal telling them they are full,
worth about 1.9 kg per copy. The twist is where it is concentrated: assistance
dogs carry it at nearly four times the rate of pets, because people breeding
for trainability were unknowingly selecting for appetite. Those dogs are not
overweight. Same gene, different routine.
