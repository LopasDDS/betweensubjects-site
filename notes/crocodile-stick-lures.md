Crocodiles and alligators lie still with sticks balanced on their snouts, and
they do it mainly in the weeks when birds are collecting nest material.

The observation. Vladimir Dinets, John C. Brueggen and James D. Brueggen,
"Crocodilians use tools for hunting", Ethology Ecology & Evolution, volume 27,
issue 1, pages 74-78 - published online in late 2013 and in print in 2015.

What was seen. Mugger crocodiles in India and American alligators in Louisiana
rest at the surface with twigs and small sticks lying across their snouts and
heads, holding still. The suggestion is that the sticks work as a lure: wading
birds such as egrets and herons need nest material, and a floating stick is
worth investigating.

Why the timing is the evidence. A crocodile with a stick on its head is, on its
own, a crocodile that a stick landed on. What turns the observation into a
claim is when it happens. The behaviour appeared predominantly during the
nest-building season of the birds, and at sites in Louisiana the researchers
recorded a significant increase in stick-displaying while wading birds were
building nests. A passive accumulation of debris should not keep a calendar.

Why that is unusual. Lures are rare among predators, and the authors argue
this is the first reported case of a predator both using objects as lures and
tracking the seasonality of its prey's behaviour. Crocodilians are also not
where anyone looks for this: the expectation was a sit-and-wait ambush animal
running on reflex.

The thing the retelling leaves out. In these systematic observations, not a
single predation attempt resulted from the behaviour. Nobody watched a bird
come for a stick and get taken. Popular versions of this story go straight to
the bird being eaten; the published work does not show that, and a later paper
from the same group says so plainly - the purported goal of the tool use was
never observed. The case rests entirely on the timing.

Limits that must be stated. Much of the observation was at captive and
semi-captive sites, chosen because trees inside the enclosures are used as
rookeries by wild birds - convenient for watching, and not a wild setting.
Numbers are small and the work is observational rather than experimental: no
one manipulated stick availability or bird season to see what followed. And
"tool use" is itself disputed here, since the stick attracts prey rather than
being manipulated to process it, which is what the term usually covers.

What this must not be heard as. It is not established that crocodiles catch
birds this way, and it is not a claim about crocodilian intelligence in
general. What is on the record is a behaviour that tracks a calendar it has no
obvious reason to track, which is interesting enough on its own and is the
part worth repeating.

Accurate summary: mugger crocodiles and American alligators were observed
resting with sticks balanced on their snouts, predominantly during the weeks
when wading birds were gathering nest material, which is hard to explain as
debris landing at random - though no bird was ever observed being caught this
way, and most of the watching was done at captive sites.
