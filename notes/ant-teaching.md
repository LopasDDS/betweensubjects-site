An ant leads another ant to food, and by the formal definition of teaching,
that is what it is doing.

What teaching means here. Biologists use a definition from Tim Caro and Marc
Hauser, 1992, precisely so the word does not rest on guessing what an animal
intends. Three conditions: the individual modifies its behaviour only in the
presence of a naive observer; it pays a cost, or at least gets no immediate
benefit; and as a result the observer learns a skill or reaches a goal faster
than it would have alone. Nothing about intention, which cannot be measured.
For a long time no non-human animal had been shown to meet all three.

The study. Nigel Franks and Tom Richardson, "Teaching in tandem-running ants",
Nature, volume 439, 2006, page 153. The species is Temnothorax albipennis, a
small ant that lives in rock crevices.

The behaviour. In tandem running, an ant that knows where food or a new nest
site is leads a single nestmate there, the two of them in close contact the
whole way. It is not a trail being followed after the fact. The pair move
together, and they communicate while they move.

Why it counts as teaching. The feedback runs both ways, which is the finding
the paper rests on. The follower keeps contact by tapping the leader with its
antennae; when that contact stops the leader slows or waits, and the follower
searches for the leader. The leader travels far more slowly than it would
going alone, so the trip costs it time it would not otherwise spend. And the
follower reaches the destination sooner than it would searching by itself. All
three conditions, in an animal with about a quarter of a million neurons.

The objection. Several researchers argued the word "teaching" imports more
than the behaviour supports - that this is signalling between two individuals,
tuned by natural selection, with nothing resembling an intention to instruct
or any sense of what the other ant does not know. The reply is that this is
exactly why the Caro and Hauser definition is written as it is: it describes a
functional relationship, and an ant meeting it tells you the function does not
require the mental machinery people assumed.

Limits that must be stated. One species, in the laboratory, over short
distances. "Costs the leader time" is a cost measured in that setting, not a
full accounting of what the colony gains. And the definition itself is a
convention - a useful one, agreed in advance rather than fitted to this
result, but a convention, and the argument about whether it captures what
anyone means by teaching is still live.

What this must not be heard as. Not that ants are clever in the way the word
usually suggests, and not that a tandem run resembles a lesson. The interest
runs the other way: a behaviour we assumed needed a mind capable of modelling
another mind turns out to be achievable with almost no brain at all, which is
a fact about teaching rather than a fact about ants.

Accurate summary: a Temnothorax ant leading a nestmate to a destination slows
down for the follower, waits when contact is broken, and gets the follower
there faster than it would have arrived alone - satisfying all three parts of
the standard biological definition of teaching, the first non-human animal
shown to do so, and prompting an argument about whether the definition is the
right one.
