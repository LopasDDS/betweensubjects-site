Phineas Gage.

On 13 September 1848, near Cavendish, Vermont, a railroad construction foreman
named Phineas Gage was using a tamping iron to pack blasting powder when it
detonated. The iron — over a metre long and a little over three centimetres
across — was driven up through his left cheek, behind his eye and out through
the top of his skull, destroying much of his left frontal lobe.

He survived. He was conscious and speaking within minutes, was treated by
Dr John Martyn Harlow, and lived a further twelve years, dying in 1860 after a
series of seizures.

The popular version: the accident destroyed his personality. He became
foul-mouthed, unreliable and unemployable, and stayed that way. Harlow's
phrase, that his friends found him "no longer Gage", is the line every
textbook uses. It is the founding case for the idea that the frontal lobes
hold personality.

What the retelling leaves out. The "no longer Gage" description comes from
Harlow's 1868 account and describes the period not long after the injury. It is
a snapshot, not a verdict on the rest of his life.

Malcolm Macmillan assembled what is actually documented in An Odd Kind of Fame
(2000) and, with Matthew Lena, in "Rehabilitating Phineas Gage" (2010). Gage
went on to work for several years as a long-distance stagecoach driver in
Valparaíso, Chile — a job requiring routine, timekeeping, the handling of
money, passengers and teams of horses over difficult roads. Whatever happened
to him immediately after the accident, that is not the life of a man who could
no longer function.

Macmillan also surveyed how the case is taught and found that a great deal of
the vivid detail has no source in any primary account, and that textbook
versions contradict each other freely. Specific claims about his behaviour
should not be repeated unless Harlow actually recorded them.

The extent of the brain damage is itself uncertain. Reconstructions from the
skull by Damasio and colleagues (1994), Ratiu (2004) and Van Horn (2012) do not
agree with one another.

Accurate summary: the injury and the survival are exactly as remarkable as
they sound. The permanent ruin is not — the best-documented part of his later
life is a skilled job held down for years, and that is the half of the story
nobody tells.
