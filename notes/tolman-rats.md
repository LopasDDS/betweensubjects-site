Tolman's rats, latent learning, and a fight the textbooks report as settled.

The study: Tolman and Honzik, 1930. Food-deprived rats ran a fourteen-unit
T-maze once a day for over three weeks, in three groups. One group found food
at the end from the first day. One never found food at all. The third found
nothing for ten days, and then on the eleventh day food was placed in the end
box before the rat set off.

The result everyone quotes. The third group's errors fell and its speed rose
almost overnight once the food appeared, matching and by some measures beating
the group that had been fed from the start. Tolman's reading: those rats had
been learning the maze all along and had no reason to show it. Reward changed
performance, not learning. He called it latent learning, and in "Cognitive
Maps in Rats and Men" (1948) argued the animals were building an internal
layout of the maze rather than a chain of turns.

What the retelling gets wrong. Arthur Jensen reviewed how this is taught
across forty-eight introductory textbooks published between 1948 and 2004 and
found the same errors repeated. Textbooks say the experiment proved
reinforcement is not necessary for learning; the question was argued for
decades and never resolved. They say behavioural theories could not account
for the result; Hull and Guthrie each produced coherent stimulus-response
explanations at the time. They attribute to Skinner the claim that
reinforcement is necessary for learning, which he did not make. And they
present the cognitive map as the only available explanation without
mentioning that it was disputed.

The detail that complicates the story. Over the first five days the error
rate fell in all three groups, including the one that never found food at
all. Something was being learned without reward everywhere in the experiment,
which makes the clean two-group morality tale harder to tell.

And the follow-up failed. The study the cognitive map is really named for —
Tolman, Ritchie and Kalish (1946), where rats appeared to take a direct
short-cut toward a goal from a new starting point — could not be replicated.

Limits that must be stated. This is one maze, one laboratory, 1930, with the
statistical conventions of its time. The Tolman-versus-Hull argument is
usually reported as a victory and is better described as a stalemate: no side
produced the decisive result, and the field moved on rather than concluding.

What this must not be heard as. Not that Tolman faked anything or that latent
learning is a myth — the overnight change in that third group is real data,
and it still needs explaining. Nor that cognitive maps turned out to be
nonsense: place cells in the hippocampus were discovered decades later and
the work on the brain's spatial system won a Nobel Prize in 2014. But that is
a separate line of evidence from neuroscience, not a vindication of a 1946
maze study that nobody could repeat. The narrow point is about how a live,
unfinished argument gets taught as a finished one.

Accurate summary: rats that ran a maze for ten days with no reward looked
like they had learned nothing, then performed almost perfectly the day after
food appeared — which Tolman read as learning that had been happening
invisibly all along. The textbook version has this defeating behaviourism;
in fact rival explanations existed, the argument ended in a stalemate, and
the experiment the phrase "cognitive map" comes from failed to replicate.
