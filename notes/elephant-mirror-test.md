An elephant passed the mirror test only after someone built a mirror big
enough to stand in front of.

The test. The mark test, devised by Gordon Gallup in 1970, is the standard
operational check for mirror self-recognition. An animal is given long access
to a mirror, then marked somewhere on its body it cannot see directly. If it
uses the reflection to investigate the mark on itself, rather than treating
the reflection as another animal, it is scored as recognising itself. Humans,
chimpanzees, orangutans and bottlenose dolphins had passed. Elephants had not.

The earlier failures. Elephants were tested with mirrors before and showed
nothing. In those studies the mirror was small and placed outside the
enclosure, out of reach. An animal the size of an elephant cannot see much of
itself in a panel it can only view from a distance and cannot walk around.

The study. Joshua Plotnik, Frans de Waal and Diana Reiss, "Self-recognition in
an Asian elephant", Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, volume
103, number 45, 2006. Three Asian elephants at the Bronx Zoo - Happy, Maxine
and Patty - were given a mirror about two and a half metres square, fixed
inside the enclosure where they could touch it, reach behind it and stand
square in front of it.

What happened first. All three used the mirror in ways that are hard to
explain as treating it as a stranger. They inspected behind it. They brought
food to it. They made repetitive movements while watching, and put their
trunks inside their own mouths while looking - investigating parts of
themselves they could not otherwise see. None of them greeted or threatened
the reflection the way an elephant greets another elephant.

The mark. Each elephant then got a visible white mark on one side of the head
and, on the other side, a colourless sham mark applied the same way. The sham
is the control: it rules out the animal simply feeling something on its skin
and touching it. Happy repeatedly touched the visible mark with her trunk
while standing at the mirror, and did not touch the sham. Maxine and Patty did
not touch either.

So the honest result is one elephant out of three.

Limits that must be stated. Three animals, one pass. Failing the mark test is
weak evidence: it requires the animal to care about a mark on its body, and an
uninterested or unmotivated animal fails for reasons that have nothing to do
with self-recognition. The test is also built around vision, which is not an
elephant's primary sense - they lead with smell and touch - so a species could
in principle recognise itself and still score zero here. Gallup's test measures
one specific behaviour, not a general capacity, and what exactly it measures
has been argued over since 1970.

What this must not be heard as. Passing the mark test is not the same as
self-awareness in the sense people usually mean, and it says nothing about
what an elephant experiences. Nor is the point that researchers were careless.
The useful lesson runs the other way: a negative result can be a fact about
the apparatus rather than the animal, and in this case the obvious fix - a
mirror an elephant could actually use - changed the answer.

Accurate summary: elephants were scored as failing the mirror test until a
2006 study gave three Asian elephants a mirror large enough and close enough
to use properly; all three behaved as though investigating themselves, and one
of them, Happy, touched a mark on her own head that she could only have seen
in the reflection, while ignoring an identically applied invisible control
mark.
