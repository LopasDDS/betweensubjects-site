Eight octopuses were handled by two strangers for two weeks, and started
treating them differently.

The study. Roland C. Anderson, Jennifer A. Mather, Mathieu Q. Monette and
Stephanie R. M. Zimsen, "Octopuses (Enteroctopus dofleini) recognize
individual humans", Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, volume 13,
issue 3, 2010, pages 261-272. The work was done at the Seattle Aquarium on
eight giant Pacific octopuses.

What they did. Each octopus was exposed to two unfamiliar people over two
weeks, under consistently different circumstances. One person always fed the
animal. The other always touched it with a bristly stick - unpleasant, not
harmful. Neither person had any prior history with the animals.

What they measured. Not a judgement call about whether the octopus seemed
pleased, but four specific things: how often a dark bar appeared across the
eyes, a body pattern octopuses produce under particular conditions; whether
the animal reached its arms toward the person or away; which way it aimed its
funnel, the muscular siphon it uses to jet water; and how fast it was
breathing.

What they found. The dark eyebar showed up significantly more often for one of
the two people than the other. Arm movements and funnel direction also differed
between the two. Respiration rate differed significantly too, but only in the
four larger octopuses - the four smaller ones showed no reliable difference.

So the honest result is a consistent difference in behaviour toward two people
who differed only in how they had treated the animal, on several independent
measures, in a species with no social life to speak of and no reason anyone
expected to find it.

Limits that must be stated. Eight animals, and the respiration effect held in
only half of them. These were aquarium octopuses, accustomed to people, in a
setting where humans appear constantly. The study did not identify what cue
the octopuses were actually using to tell the two apart, which means "they
recognised the person" is a description of the outcome rather than an
explanation of it. The journal is a specialist welfare publication rather than
a general cognition venue, and the work has not been widely replicated.

What this must not be heard as. "Recognition" here means the animal reliably
behaved differently toward two individuals. It is not a claim that an octopus
holds a concept of a person, remembers a face, or bears a grudge. It is also
not evidence about octopus consciousness, which is a separate and much harder
argument that this experiment does not touch.

Accurate summary: eight giant Pacific octopuses at the Seattle Aquarium met
two unfamiliar people over two weeks, one who always fed them and one who
always prodded them with a bristly stick, and came to respond differently to
the two - in body pattern, arm movement and funnel direction, with a breathing
difference in the four largest animals only.
