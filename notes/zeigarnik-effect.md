The Zeigarnik effect.

Bluma Zeigarnik, a Lithuanian-Soviet psychologist working in Kurt Lewin's group
in Berlin, published the study in 1927 in Psychologische Forschung.

Participants were given a series of short tasks. Some were allowed to finish;
others were interrupted before completion. Afterwards they were asked to recall
the tasks they had been given. Interrupted tasks were recalled considerably
more often than completed ones.

The origin story usually told is that Lewin noticed a waiter who could recall
unpaid orders in detail but forgot them once the bill was settled. This is an
anecdote repeated in secondary sources, not a recorded experiment, and details
such as the city vary between tellings. It may be told, but only as a story
about how the question arose.

Replication has been mixed. Later attempts have sometimes found the effect and
sometimes not, and its size appears to depend on how motivated the person was
to finish the task and how the interruption was framed.

Accurate summary: unfinished tasks do tend to stay more accessible in memory,
but the effect is more conditional than its popular framing suggests.
