Ego depletion, and the replication that could not find it.

The original claim: Roy Baumeister and colleagues, "Ego Depletion: Is the
Active Self a Limited Resource?" (Journal of Personality and Social
Psychology, 1998). Participants who had to resist eating chocolate biscuits,
and instead ate radishes, later gave up sooner on an unsolvable puzzle than
participants who had not had to resist anything.

The interpretation: self-control draws on a limited resource that depletes with
use, like a muscle tiring. It became one of the most cited ideas in
psychology, and produced a large literature — including work suggesting the
resource was literally glucose, and that a sugary drink could restore it.

What happened next.

Hagger and colleagues ran a pre-registered multi-laboratory replication
published in Perspectives on Psychological Science in 2016. Twenty-three
laboratories, over two thousand participants, one agreed protocol chosen in
advance and approved by proponents of the effect. The pooled estimate came out
close to zero.

A second large-scale attempt, Vohs and colleagues (Psychological Science,
2021), ran a further pre-registered multi-site test with a different task and
also found the effect very small or absent.

Publication bias is the likeliest explanation for the original literature. The
effect appeared robust because studies that found it were published and
studies that did not were mostly not, so a meta-analysis of the published
record measured what got printed rather than what was true.

The glucose version has fared worse still. The idea that self-control is
limited by blood sugar, and restorable by a sweet drink, does not survive
scrutiny — the brain's glucose consumption does not vary nearly enough to
support it, and the drink experiments have not replicated reliably.

What may not be said. That self-control does not exist, or that fatigue is
imaginary. People do perform worse when tired, bored, or unmotivated, and that
is not in dispute. What is in dispute is the specific claim that exerting
self-control drains a finite reserve that then impairs unrelated tasks.

Baumeister has not accepted the null result and has contested the replication's
choice of task. That disagreement is live and should be described as such
rather than as a settled defeat.

Accurate summary: an idea that shaped how a generation was told to manage
their willpower was tested by twenty-three laboratories at once, and the
effect came out at roughly nothing.
