A fish behaved as though it had noticed a mark on its own throat, and the
argument that followed is about what the test measures at all.

The test being used. In the mark test, an animal is marked somewhere it cannot
see directly, then given a mirror. Touching the mark on its own body is scored
as recognising the reflection as itself. Great apes pass. Most species do not.

The study. Masanori Kohda and colleagues, "If a fish can pass the mark test,
what are the implications for consciousness and self-awareness testing in
animals?", PLOS Biology, 2019. The subject was the cleaner wrasse, Labroides
dimidiatus - a small reef fish that makes its living picking parasites off
other fish.

What they did. A coloured elastomer was injected under the skin, leaving a
visible mark, in some cases on the throat. The fish were then given a mirror.
The controls were careful: a colourless sham mark, an invisible mark, and a
condition with no mirror at all.

What they found. Marked fish spent a long time at the mirror and held
themselves so the marked side faced it. They then scraped the marked spot
against the substrate - throat-scraping after a throat mark. They did not do
this with an invisible mark, and they did not do it without a mirror. The
authors argued this meets the criteria of the mark test.

The objection, from the primer published alongside it. Frans de Waal argued
the result falls short of mirror self-recognition, for two reasons worth
stating precisely.

First, the behaviour is ambiguous. The whole case rests on one stereotyped
act, self-scraping, which is a fixed action pattern in many fish. And this
species, of all species, makes a living reacting to parasites on other fish -
so a wrasse seeing a marked fish in a mirror may be responding to an
apparently parasitised neighbour rather than to itself. De Waal argued that a
behaviour never seen in the absence of a mirror would be far stronger evidence.

Second, the mark is felt as well as seen. An injection under the skin is
likely to be irritating. The sham mark controls for touch alone, but not for
the combination of a strong physical sensation with a visual mark. De Waal
called this a "Felt Mark Test", noting that rhesus macaques show self-directed
behaviour at a mirror only after that kind of combined stimulation - which
would place the wrasse nearer monkeys than apes.

What he proposed instead. Not a verdict but a change of scale: drop the
pass/fail split and treat mirror understanding as graded, with intermediate
levels such as using a mirror as a tool or simply not treating the reflection
as a stranger. Kohda's group has since run further experiments answering the
methodological points, and has itself argued that either the criteria for
self-awareness or the mark test needs rethinking.

Limits that must be stated. Few fish, one species, and a mark delivered by
injection. The sample size was among the published criticisms. The central
dispute is not really about this experiment's execution - it is about whether
a single scraping behaviour can carry the weight the mark test puts on it.

What this must not be heard as. Not "fish are self-aware", and not "the study
was debunked". Two groups of serious people agree on what the fish did and
disagree about what it shows, which is the normal state of a good result and
is usually lost in the retelling.

Accurate summary: cleaner wrasse given a visible mark under the skin spent
time at a mirror positioned to show it, then scraped that spot on the
substrate - doing neither with an invisible mark or without a mirror. Whether
that is self-recognition, a parasite-removal reflex aimed at an apparent
neighbour, or a response to a mark they could feel as well as see, is
unresolved and argued in print.
