The Dunning-Kruger effect.

Justin Kruger and David Dunning, "Unskilled and Unaware of It: How Difficulties
in Recognizing One's Own Incompetence Lead to Inflated Self-Assessments"
(Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1999).

What the paper reported: across tests of humour, logical reasoning and grammar,
participants who scored in the bottom quarter substantially overestimated their
own percentile rank, while the highest scorers slightly underestimated theirs.

The popular version is that incompetent people are too incompetent to notice,
usually stated as the less someone knows, the more certain they are. It is
almost always illustrated with a curve rising to a peak of confidence, often
labelled Mount Stupid, falling into a valley of despair and climbing again.

That curve appears nowhere in the 1999 paper. There is no such graph in the
research. It was drawn later for the internet, and it is not a finding.

The stronger objection is statistical. Because self-assessments are noisy and
imperfectly related to actual scores, comparing the two produces this pattern
even when nobody is systematically deluded: the lowest scorers have nowhere to
go but up and the highest nowhere to go but down. That is regression to the
mean, combined with most people rating themselves a little above average.
Krueger and Mueller raised this in 2002. Nuhfer and colleagues (2016, 2017),
using measures designed to avoid the problem, did not find the classic pattern.
Gignac and Zajenkowski argued in Intelligence (2020) that the effect is mostly
a statistical artefact. A standard demonstration is that randomly generated
data, containing no psychology at all, reproduces the familiar chart.

Dunning has responded that the phenomenon survives these criticisms, and the
argument is not settled. It should be described as a live dispute, not as a
finding that has been withdrawn.

What is not in dispute is that people are poor judges of their own competence
in both directions, and that experts frequently underrate themselves relative
to others.

Accurate summary: the study is real, the effect is contested, and the chart
everyone shares was never part of it.
