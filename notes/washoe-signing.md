Washoe, the signing chimpanzees, and the man who refuted his own experiment.

The work: Allen and Beatrix Gardner began teaching American Sign Language to
a young chimpanzee named Washoe at the University of Nevada, Reno, in the
late 1960s. She was cross-fostered — raised in a human household rather than
a cage — and by the end she was credited with roughly 350 signs. She later
passed signs to her adopted son, Loulis, without human teaching.

The Gardners were not casual about counting. A sign was only added to
Washoe's vocabulary after she had produced it spontaneously rather than in
response to a prompt, appropriately, and in the right context, on every day
for at least fourteen consecutive days, with double-blind scoring. That
standard is stricter than most of what was later done in the field and it is
the reason the work was taken seriously.

The claim that travelled. What reached the public was larger than the
vocabulary: that a chimpanzee had acquired language the way a child does,
including combining signs into something like sentences. Washoe signing
"water bird" at a swan is the example everyone repeats.

The refutation, from inside. Herbert Terrace raised a chimpanzee he named Nim
Chimpsky — a joke at Noam Chomsky's expense — expecting to demonstrate exactly
that. He analysed more than nineteen thousand of Nim's multi-sign utterances
and found apparent structure: particular signs tending to occupy particular
positions, in a way that looked too regular to be habit. Then he went back to
the videotapes. Most of Nim's utterances turned out to be prompted by what the
teacher had just signed a moment earlier. The structure was in the
conversation, not in the chimpanzee. Terrace and his colleagues published this
against their own project in Science in 1979, under the title "Can an ape
create a sentence?" Their answer was no. Laura-Ann Petitto, who had worked
most closely with Nim, was one of the authors.

Limits that must be stated. Terrace studied Nim, not Washoe, and the Gardners
and Roger Fouts disputed that the finding carried across — Nim was handled by
a large rotating cast of teachers in conditions nothing like Washoe's
household, which is a fair objection. "Water bird" is a single anecdote and
cannot settle anything either way: it may be a coinage, or simply the sign for
water followed by the sign for bird. And the dispute is about grammar, not
about whether the animals learned anything.

What this must not be heard as. Not that the apes were faking, not that the
researchers were dishonest, and not that chimpanzees learned nothing. They
demonstrably learned to use symbols to ask for things and to label them,
which was not obvious beforehand and is still interesting. What did not
survive is the specific claim that they combined those symbols into sentences
the way a child does. And Terrace did not expose someone else: he set out to
prove the claim, found he had produced an artefact, and said so in print.

Accurate summary: chimpanzees taught sign language really did learn hundreds
of signs and use them to name and request — but the headline claim, that they
were building sentences, collapsed when the researcher trying to prove it
watched his own videotapes and found the apes were mostly echoing whatever
their teacher had just signed.
