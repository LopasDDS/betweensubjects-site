Pavlov's dogs, and the bell that mostly was not there.

Ivan Pavlov was a Russian physiologist. He won the Nobel Prize in 1904 for his
work on the physiology of digestion, not for conditioning, which came out of
that earlier work.

What is true: dogs in his laboratory came to salivate in response to a signal
that had been repeatedly paired with food, and he measured this precisely,
collecting saliva through a surgically implanted fistula and timing and
quantifying the response. Classical conditioning is real and the experiments
were meticulous.

The bell is the part that is wrong. The image everybody carries — Pavlov
ringing a handbell and the dog drooling — is largely an invention of
popularisation and translation. His laboratory used metronomes, buzzers,
whistles, tones, a harmonium, flashing lights and touch as conditioned
stimuli. Daniel Todes, in Ivan Pavlov: A Russian Life in Science (2014), traces
how the bell entered the English-language telling. A bell is in fact a poor
instrument for this work, because its pitch and volume cannot be held steady
between trials, and precise control was the whole point of the apparatus.

It may be said that a bell appears occasionally in the literature. What may not
be said is that Pavlov's experiments were built on ringing a bell.

The second distortion is in what "Pavlovian" has come to mean. It is used as a
byword for mindless reflex, but Pavlov's own interest was in the nervous
system's regulation of digestion, and the work was a piece of careful
physiology rather than a demonstration that animals are automatic.

Accurate summary: the science is sound and the dogs are real. The bell is a
detail the retelling invented, and it is the only part most people can
remember.
