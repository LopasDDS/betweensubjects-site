The horse that remembers your face was angry, and the statistical error in the
study before it.

The first claim, 2016. Amy Smith, Leanne Proops, Karen McComb and colleagues,
"Functionally relevant responses to human facial expressions of emotion in the
domestic horse", Biology Letters. Twenty-eight horses were shown photographs of
human faces, angry or happy, with no training beforehand. The reported findings
were a left-gaze bias to the angry photographs - animals tend to look at
threatening things with the left eye, because the left eye feeds the right
hemisphere - and a faster rise in heart rate to angry faces than to happy ones
(median 14.7 seconds against 29). The experimenters could not see which
photograph they were holding up, which matters on this subject more than most.

The challenge, the same year. Peter Schmoll published a re-analysis in the same
journal: "Can horses read emotional cues from human faces?" His objection to the
gaze result is a genuine and common statistical mistake. The authors had shown
that the laterality index differed significantly from zero for angry faces and
did not for happy ones - and treated that as showing the two differed from each
other. It does not. A significant result in one group and a non-significant one
in another is not a test of the difference between the groups; that comparison
has to be made directly. He also questioned heart-rate latencies under a
second, which are too fast to be a response to what was on the card. The
authors published a reply defending the functional relevance of the findings.
The exchange is on the record and was not settled by either side.

The stronger result, 2018. Proops, McComb and colleagues, "Animals remember
previous facial expressions that specific humans have exhibited", Current
Biology. A horse is shown a photograph of a person's face, angry or happy. Some
hours later, that same person walks in - in the flesh, with a neutral
expression, and not knowing which photograph the horse had been shown. Horses
that had seen the angry photograph looked at the person more with the left eye:
the threat-processing side. The effect was specific to that individual. A
different person did not get it.

Why the blinding is the point. The person meeting the horse did not know which
photograph it had seen, so they could not behave differently and be read. That
is the Clever Hans control, built in from the start - a horse in Berlin appeared
to do arithmetic in 1904 and was in fact reading the questioner's involuntary
posture, and the fix has been the same ever since. Here it is what makes the
finding mean anything.

Limits that must be stated. Twenty-eight horses in 2016, and a single laboratory
group behind both papers. The 2018 measure is gaze direction, which is an
inference about what the horse is attending to, not a readout of what it feels.
"Perceived more negatively" is the authors' interpretation of a looking bias, and
a looking bias is a small behaviour. Whether the 2016 result survives Schmoll's
objection is still an open argument, and the 2018 study is not a replication of
it - different design, different measure.

What this must not be heard as. Not that horses read minds, feel human emotions,
or know you are in a bad mood. Not that the 2016 authors were sloppy - the
statistical error Schmoll names is one of the most common in published science
and gets past reviewers constantly. And not that the dispute means the whole
thing collapsed: the 2018 design is the stronger one and it stands on its own.

Accurate summary: show a horse a photograph of an angry stranger, and hours
later, when that stranger walks in with a neutral face, the horse looks at them
with the eye it uses for threats - and only at them. The study behind the
headline before it was picked apart for a real statistical error: proving a
result in one condition and failing to prove it in another is not proof the two
differ.
