The bee dance that won a Nobel Prize, and the man who spent his career saying
it meant nothing.

The finding. Karl von Frisch worked out that a returning honeybee performs a
figure-of-eight on the comb whose straight middle run encodes two things: the
angle of the run against vertical gives the direction of the food relative to
the sun, and the duration of the waggle gives the distance. He was awarded the
Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1973, shared with Konrad Lorenz and
Niko Tinbergen. It is one of the few cases where an animal was shown to be
communicating something abstract - a place that is not here - to another
animal.

The challenge, which was serious. From 1967 Adrian Wenner, later with Patrick
Wells, argued that the dance was a delusion of the observer. His case was not
that bees do not dance; it was that the other bees do not use the information.
Bees, he said, find food by smell - the odour the discoverer carries on her
body and leaves at the site. And when Wenner ran the experiments with tighter
controls against odour cues, recruits could not find the food from the dance
alone. That is a real result, not a complaint. He and Wells published in Nature
in 1973, the same year von Frisch took the prize.

What happened to Wenner. He was largely frozen out: for years the editors of
major journals would not take his results, and he withdrew from active bee
research. Whatever one concludes about the science, that is what happened to
the dissenter while the consensus collected a Nobel.

How it was settled, thirty-eight years later. Riley, Greggers, Smith, Reynolds
and Menzel, "The flight paths of honeybees recruited by the waggle dance",
Nature, 2005. They glued harmonic radar transponders to bees that had attended
a dance and tracked the actual flight paths - the thing nobody had ever been
able to see.

The manipulation that decided it. Some recruits were caught as they left the
hive and released up to 250 metres away. If they were following a smell, they
would have gone to the food. They did not. They flew the vector the dance had
given them - the direction and distance that would have been correct from the
hive - and ended up in the wrong place, displaced by exactly the amount the
experimenters had moved them. They were carrying an instruction, not following
a trail.

Where it actually landed, which is not a clean win. The accepted picture now is
that both mechanisms operate: the dance carries recruits to the right area, and
odour does the last part, homing them onto the flowers. Von Frisch was right
that the dance conveys location. Wenner was right that odour matters and that
the early controls for it were not good enough. The textbook version drops the
second half.

Limits that must be stated. The 2005 study is one study, and radar tracking
constrains how far and where you can work. It settled the specific question of
whether recruits use the vector, not every question about what bees attend to.
And "language" is doing a lot of work as a word here - conveying a direction and
a distance is not the same thing as a grammar.

What this must not be heard as. Not that Wenner was a crank; his odour
experiments were competent and his objection forced a level of control the
field had not applied to itself. Not that von Frisch fabricated anything. And
not that consensus is usually wrong - it was substantially right here. The
uncomfortable part is narrower: the objection was reasonable, it took nearly
four decades and a technology nobody had in 1967 to answer it properly, and in
the meantime the person raising it could not get published.

Accurate summary: von Frisch decoded the honeybee waggle dance and won a Nobel
for it, while a rival spent decades arguing the bees were just following a
smell. In 2005 researchers put radar transponders on recruits, displaced them
250 metres, and watched them fly the exact wrong-but-correct vector the dance
had specified. Both men turned out to have half of it.
