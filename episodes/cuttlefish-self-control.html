Did you know? Six cuttlefish waited two minutes for a better meal

Cuttlefish refused an easy meal to wait for a better one, and the ones that waited longest were also the fastest learners. · published 20 October 2026

Watch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

Six cuttlefish declined an immediately available piece of prawn in order to get a live grass shrimp they preferred, maintaining delays of roughly 50 to 130 seconds, and the individuals that waited longest were also the quickest to learn and re-learn a colour association - in an animal with none of the traits usually invoked to explain self-control.

The angle

Self-control in animals is normally explained by tool use, food caching or social life — and a cuttlefish has none of the three, which is why this study matters more than the waiting itself: the individuals that held out longest, up to roughly 130 seconds, were also the fastest to learn a colour association and the fastest to notice when the rule flipped, a link never before shown outside primates, in an ambush hunter for whom staying still until something good enough passes is simply the job.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

The task. Delay of gratification: an animal is offered something good now, or something better if it waits. It is the shape of the marshmallow experiments in children, and self-control of this kind had mostly been looked for in primates and a few birds, where it is usually explained by tool use, food caching or social life.

The study. Alexandra Schnell, Markus Boeckle, Micaela Rivera, Nicola Clayton and Roger Hanlon, “Cuttlefish exert self-control in a delay of gratification task”, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, volume 288, 2021.

What they did. Six common cuttlefish, Sepia officinalis, were shown two foods in separate clear chambers. A piece of king prawn was available immediately. A live grass shrimp - the preferred food - could only be eaten after a wait. The delay started at 10 seconds and was increased in steps of 10.

What happened. All six waited for the shrimp and left the prawn alone. The most self-controlled individuals held out up to about 130 seconds, with the reported range of maintained delays running from 50 to 130 seconds. Some turned their bodies away from the prawn while waiting, which is suggestive rather than evidence.

The second finding. In a separate task the same animals learned that one of two markers, grey or white, was the rewarded one, and then the reward was switched to the other. The cuttlefish that learned the association faster and noticed the switch faster were the same ones that had waited longest. That link between self-control and learning had not been shown before outside the primates.

Why it is odd. The usual explanations for self-control do not apply here. Cuttlefish do not use tools, do not cache food, and are not social. The authors suggest it may instead be a by-product of how they hunt: an animal that spends its life camouflaged and still, striking only when something good enough passes, is an animal for which waiting is the job.

Limits

Six animals, and two more were recruited and refused to take part at all - which is a small sample before any attrition. The link between waiting and learning is reported as a relationship across six individuals, which is very few to carry a correlation. And the accounts of the method differ: the Cambridge release describes a king prawn as the immediate option, while other summaries describe an Asian shore crab and markers on the chamber doors, so the published paper should be the reference for any detail that matters.

What this does not show

Not that a cuttlefish is planning, or imagining the shrimp it is about to get. The task measures whether an animal will forgo something available for something better, which is a behaviour, and the paper does not claim more. The comparison to children is a comparison of procedure, not of what is going on inside.
