Why unfinished tasks stick — but less reliably than told

The Zeigarnik effect. · published 1 September 2026

Watch on FacebookWatch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

Unfinished tasks do tend to stay more accessible in memory, but the effect is more conditional than its popular framing suggests.

The angle

Bluma Zeigarnik’s 1927 study found interrupted tasks were recalled more often than completed ones — but the modern productivity version oversells it, because later replications only find the effect when the person actually wanted to finish and the interruption felt like an interruption rather than a release.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

Bluma Zeigarnik, a Lithuanian-Soviet psychologist working in Kurt Lewin’s group in Berlin, published the study in 1927 in Psychologische Forschung.

Participants were given a series of short tasks. Some were allowed to finish; others were interrupted before completion. Afterwards they were asked to recall the tasks they had been given. Interrupted tasks were recalled considerably more often than completed ones.

The origin story usually told is that Lewin noticed a waiter who could recall unpaid orders in detail but forgot them once the bill was settled. This is an anecdote repeated in secondary sources, not a recorded experiment, and details such as the city vary between tellings. It may be told, but only as a story about how the question arose.

Replication has been mixed. Later attempts have sometimes found the effect and sometimes not, and its size appears to depend on how motivated the person was to finish the task and how the interruption was framed.
