Did you know? A penguin huddle moves in waves of 5 centimetres

An emperor penguin huddle moves in waves, and the steps are tiny on purpose. · published 23 October 2026

Watch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

Time-lapse images of an emperor penguin huddle at minus 43 degrees show it standing still for 30 to 60 seconds at a time and then moving in a travelling wave, each bird stepping only 5 to 10 centimetres - small enough that the huddle stays at its maximum packing density of about 21 birds per square metre while continuously reorganising itself.

The angle

Time-lapse of an emperor penguin huddle at minus 43 degrees shows it holding still for 30 to 60 seconds and then moving as a travelling wave, each bird stepping only 5 to 10 centimetres — steps too small to open a gap, which is exactly why the crowd flows instead of jamming, and no bird has to cooperate or take turns for it to work.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

The problem. Emperor penguins winter on sea ice in temperatures that reach around minus 43 degrees, and they survive it by huddling. The huddle has to be as tightly packed as possible - around 21 birds per square metre - because the packing is the insulation. But a bird on the windward edge needs to get to the middle eventually, and the middle gets too warm, so the whole structure has to circulate. A tightly packed crowd that needs to rearrange itself is exactly the situation that produces a jam.

The study. Daniel P. Zitterbart, Barbara Wienecke, James P. Butler and Ben Fabry, “Coordinated movements prevent jamming in an Emperor penguin huddle”, PLoS ONE, volume 6, number 6, 2011, article e20260. The data are time-lapse images taken every 1.3 seconds over four hours of an Antarctic winter at Dronning Maud Land.

What the images show. The huddle is almost entirely still. Then, every 30 to 60 seconds, it moves - and the movement is a travelling wave. Birds at one edge take a step, the ones behind them follow a moment later, and the displacement passes through the group the way a Mexican wave passes through a stadium crowd.

The size of the step is the finding. Each bird moves only about 5 to 10 centimetres - roughly half a bird’s width or less. That is too small to open a gap anyone could exploit and too small to disturb the birds in the middle, and it is small enough that the huddle never loosens. Larger steps would do what larger steps do in any dense crowd: open voids, create pressure, and jam. Small coordinated ones let a packed solid of birds flow slowly without ever ceasing to be packed.

What it achieves. The huddle keeps maximum density while reorganising continuously, drifting as a whole and gradually cycling birds through positions, without anyone having to push.

Limits

This is four hours of one huddle in one location, observed and not manipulated - nobody could vary step size and see the huddle jam. The wave is described from image tracking rather than from following named individuals, so “a bird works its way to the middle” is an inference about the structure rather than a path anyone traced. And the mechanism is described, not explained: what makes a penguin take its step when it does is not established here.

What this does not show

Not that penguins are cooperating in any deliberate sense, or taking turns out of fairness - a persistent misreading of huddling. Each bird is trying to be warmer. The order comes out of a lot of cold animals pressed together, each making a very small move, which is a more interesting result than altruism would be.
