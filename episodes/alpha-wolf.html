Alpha wolf: the biologist who spent 20 years taking it back

The alpha wolf, and the biologist who spent decades taking it back. · published 11 September 2026

Watch on FacebookWatch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

The alpha wolf was described in unrelated captive wolves crammed into a zoo enclosure in 1947; wild packs turned out to be families led by the parents, with no rank to fight for, and the biologist whose book made the term famous argued against it for decades and could not get that book out of print until 2022.

The angle

The alpha wolf was described in 1947 among unrelated wolves crammed into a zoo enclosure about ten by twenty metres, where strangers really do fight over rank — but field observation of wild packs found families led by the breeding parents, with no contest to win, and L. David Mech, whose 1970 book carried the word to the public, argued in print against it in 1999 and still could not get the book withdrawn until 2022.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

Where it came from. In 1947 the Swiss ethologist Rudolf Schenkel published “Expression Studies on Wolves” (Ausdrucks-Studien an Wolfen) in the journal Behaviour, based on wolves at Basel Zoo. The enclosure was roughly ten metres by twenty, and held up to ten wolves. They were not a family. They were unrelated adults collected from different facilities and put together in a space a fraction of the range a wild wolf uses. In those conditions the animals fought over position, and Schenkel described what he saw: a hierarchy with a top male and a top female. He called them alpha.

How it spread. L. David Mech’s 1970 book “The Wolf: The Ecology and Behavior of an Endangered Species” was the work that carried the term to a general readership. Mech did not invent it — that was Schenkel’s language — but his book is why anyone outside ethology has heard it.

What field observation found instead. Watching wild wolves rather than caged ones, Mech found the pack is not a group of rivals who fought for rank. It is a family. In his own words, most wolves who lead packs “achieved their position simply by mating and producing pups, which then became their pack”. The leaders are the parents, and the rest are their offspring. There is no contest to win because there are no strangers to beat.

The correction. In 1999 Mech published “Alpha status, dominance, and division of labor in wolf packs” in the Canadian Journal of Zoology, arguing directly that alpha should be retired for wild wolves. He says the word itself is the problem: “’Alpha’ implies competing with others and becoming top dog by winning a contest or battle.” He now uses breeding male and breeding female, or simply male parent and female parent.

The part that makes this unusual. Mech spent years asking his own publisher to stop printing the 1970 book, because so much of it was outdated — the alpha material above all. The publisher kept it in print. It was finally withdrawn in 2022, more than fifty years after publication and more than twenty after he had argued against its central idea in print.

Limits

Schenkel was not hallucinating and Mech was not careless. Unrelated wolves confined together really do form dominance hierarchies; that is a true description of an artificial situation, and the error was generalising it to animals living free. Wolves are not free of social structure either — in the uncommon case of a pack with more than one breeding pair, Mech himself still uses “dominant breeder” and “subordinate breeder”. The claim is narrow: the fighting-for-rank picture describes captivity, not a wild pack.

What this does not show

It says nothing at all about human social hierarchies. “Alpha male” applied to people was a borrowing from wild wolves of something that was only ever observed in captive ones — a distortion of a distortion — and it had no support from this research even during the decades when the wolf version was believed. Nor is the lesson that scientists are unreliable: a researcher arguing in public against the most famous idea in his own bestselling book, for twenty years, is the process working.
