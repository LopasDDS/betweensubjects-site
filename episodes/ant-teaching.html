Did you know? The first animal to pass the teaching test was an ant

An ant leads another ant to food, and by the formal definition of teaching, that is what it is doing. · published 19 October 2026

Watch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

A Temnothorax ant leading a nestmate to a destination slows down for the follower, waits when contact is broken, and gets the follower there faster than it would have arrived alone - satisfying all three parts of the standard biological definition of teaching, the first non-human animal shown to do so, and prompting an argument about whether the definition is the right one.

The angle

Biologists defined teaching by three measurable conditions — you alter your behaviour only when a naive observer is present, you pay a cost for it, and the observer learns faster than it would alone — and a rock-crevice ant leading a single nestmate to food meets all three: it slows far below its solo pace, waits when the follower’s antennae stop tapping it, and gets that follower there sooner, which says less about how clever ants are than about how little machinery teaching apparently requires.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

What teaching means here. Biologists use a definition from Tim Caro and Marc Hauser, 1992, precisely so the word does not rest on guessing what an animal intends. Three conditions: the individual modifies its behaviour only in the presence of a naive observer; it pays a cost, or at least gets no immediate benefit; and as a result the observer learns a skill or reaches a goal faster than it would have alone. Nothing about intention, which cannot be measured. For a long time no non-human animal had been shown to meet all three.

The study. Nigel Franks and Tom Richardson, “Teaching in tandem-running ants”, Nature, volume 439, 2006, page 153. The species is Temnothorax albipennis, a small ant that lives in rock crevices.

The behaviour. In tandem running, an ant that knows where food or a new nest site is leads a single nestmate there, the two of them in close contact the whole way. It is not a trail being followed after the fact. The pair move together, and they communicate while they move.

Why it counts as teaching. The feedback runs both ways, which is the finding the paper rests on. The follower keeps contact by tapping the leader with its antennae; when that contact stops the leader slows or waits, and the follower searches for the leader. The leader travels far more slowly than it would going alone, so the trip costs it time it would not otherwise spend. And the follower reaches the destination sooner than it would searching by itself. All three conditions, in an animal with about a quarter of a million neurons.

The objection. Several researchers argued the word “teaching” imports more than the behaviour supports - that this is signalling between two individuals, tuned by natural selection, with nothing resembling an intention to instruct or any sense of what the other ant does not know. The reply is that this is exactly why the Caro and Hauser definition is written as it is: it describes a functional relationship, and an ant meeting it tells you the function does not require the mental machinery people assumed.

Limits

One species, in the laboratory, over short distances. “Costs the leader time” is a cost measured in that setting, not a full accounting of what the colony gains. And the definition itself is a convention - a useful one, agreed in advance rather than fitted to this result, but a convention, and the argument about whether it captures what anyone means by teaching is still live.

What this does not show

Not that ants are clever in the way the word usually suggests, and not that a tandem run resembles a lesson. The interest runs the other way: a behaviour we assumed needed a mind capable of modelling another mind turns out to be achievable with almost no brain at all, which is a fact about teaching rather than a fact about ants.
