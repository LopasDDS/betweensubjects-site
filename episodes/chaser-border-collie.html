Chaser knew 1,022 words. The grammar part is the soft edge

Chaser, the dog who knew a thousand words, and the part of the claim that is weaker than the headline. · published 16 September 2026

Watch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

A retired psychology professor spent three years teaching his border collie the individual names of 1,022 objects, and she could pick any one of them out of a pile — the largest vocabulary ever measured in an animal, and it holds up. The claim that she also understood sentence structure is the shaky one: the objects sat in predictable rooms, and her trainer was watching from across the room.

The angle

The record vocabulary of Chaser the border collie was tested and re-tested and it holds — the claim that actually wobbles is the later one, that she understood sentence structure, because in that experiment the direct objects always sat in one room and the prepositional objects in another, and her trainer was in the room watching.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

The work: John W. Pilley, Professor Emeritus of Psychology at Wofford College, began training a border collie puppy named Chaser in June 2004, when she was eight weeks old. She had been a birthday present from his wife, Sally. The central result was published by Pilley and Alliston K. Reid as “Border collie comprehends object names as verbal referents” in Behavioural Processes in 2011.

The number. Chaser learned and retained the proper-noun names of 1,022 individual objects — toys, balls, frisbees, each with its own name, not a category label. She could be asked for one by name and fetch it from a heap of the others. It is the largest tested vocabulary of any non-human animal. The training ran about three years, several hours a day, and the researchers stopped because of their own time constraints, not because Chaser had stopped learning.

What the paper actually tested, in four parts. Experiment 1 established and re-tested the 1,022 names. Experiment 2 randomly combined three commands with three names, so that she had to treat the name and the instruction as separate pieces of meaning rather than as one memorised unit. Experiment 3 showed she could also learn three common nouns — words for categories rather than individuals. Experiment 4 tested learning by exclusion: shown a group of familiar objects plus one novel one and asked for a name she had never heard, she fetched the new object.

The thing she was not the first at. Exclusion learning in a dog was already on the record. Juliane Kaminski, Josep Call and Julia Fischer published Rico, also a border collie, in Science in 2004 — over 200 items, inferring a new name by exclusion and still recalling it four weeks later. They called it fast mapping, and the point of the paper was that it did not require a language faculty specific to humans. Chaser extended the vocabulary about fivefold; she did not open the question.

The weaker claim. In 2013 Pilley published “Border collie comprehends sentences containing a prepositional object, verb, and direct object” in Learning and Motivation, reporting that Chaser could tell “to ball take Frisbee” from “to Frisbee take ball.” This is the part usually retold as a dog understanding grammar, and it is the part that does not hold as firmly. In that experiment the two direct objects were always placed in one room and the two prepositional objects always in another, so the room did much of the work that word order was credited with. And Chaser’s trainer was in the room during testing — the Clever Hans problem, the same one that the 2011 detection-dog work found in trained working animals a century after Pfungst.

Limits

One dog, one household, one trainer, and roughly three years of daily one-to-one work that almost no dog will ever get. Nothing here shows that dogs in general know a thousand words, or that border collies do. Fetching a named object on command is reference — a sound reliably picks out a thing — which is not the same as language, and Pilley and Reid did not claim it was. The exclusion result has the same open question Rico’s did: without a control for plain novelty preference, choosing the unfamiliar object need not mean inferring its name.

What this does not show

Not that Pilley was exaggerating or that the vocabulary result is in doubt — the 1,022 names were tested repeatedly and they stand. Not that dogs are secretly people. And not a debunking: the honest shape is that the big number is real and the grammar is the soft edge. Pilley died in June 2018. Chaser died in July 2019, aged fifteen.
