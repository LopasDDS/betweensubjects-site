Wolves Howl Most for Their Closest Packmate, Not Stress

Wolves howl most for the packmate they are closest to, and it is not simple distress. · published 30 September 2026

Watch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

When a wolf was walked away from its pack, the wolves left behind howled in proportion to how close they were to that particular animal and how high its rank was, not in proportion to their measured stress hormones, which suggests howling is a socially directed signal rather than a pure distress reflex.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

The study. Francesco Mazzini, Simon W. Townsend, Zsofia Viranyi and Friederike Range, “Wolf Howling Is Mediated by Relationship Quality Rather Than Underlying Emotional Stress”, Current Biology, volume 23, issue 17, 2013. The work was done at the Wolf Science Center in Ernstbrunn, Austria, on nine hand-raised captive wolves living in two packs.

What they did. One wolf at a time was taken out of the enclosure for a walk. The researchers then recorded how much each remaining wolf howled over the following twenty minutes, and took saliva samples to measure cortisol, the standard hormonal marker of stress. Because the wolves had lived together for years, the team already had detailed records of who groomed, greeted and rested with whom, and of each animal’s rank.

What they found. Two things predicted how much a wolf howled after a packmate left: how strong its affiliative bond with that particular individual was, and how high-ranking the departed wolf was. Cortisol did rise after a separation, but it did not predict how much any given wolf howled. A wolf whose close companion had gone howled a great deal whether or not its stress markers were elevated, and a stressed wolf whose companion was still present did not howl much.

Why that matters. The obvious explanation for howling after a separation is that it is an involuntary distress call, pushed out by the animal’s internal state, the way a yelp follows a shock. These results do not fit that. If howling tracked the relationship rather than the hormone, it looks less like a reflex and more like a signal aimed at a specific absent animal, which the authors argue implies some degree of flexible control over when it is produced.

Limits

Nine animals is a small sample, and they were hand-raised captive wolves in an enclosure, not a wild pack ranging over territory. The design is correlational: the researchers did not manipulate relationship quality, they measured it. Cortisol is one proxy for stress, not a complete readout of an animal’s emotional state, and a hormone measured in saliva lags behind the moment of separation. “Some degree of voluntary control” is an inference the authors draw from an absence of correlation, not something the study demonstrated directly.

What this does not show

It is not evidence that wolves howl “because they miss someone” in the sense a person means it, and the study makes no claim about what a wolf feels. It also says nothing about the full range of what howling does. Wolves howl to locate one another across distance and to advertise territory to rival packs, and those functions are not in dispute here. The narrow claim is about which variable predicted howling in this separation test.
