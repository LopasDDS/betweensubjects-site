Learned Helplessness: Its Authors’ Own 2016 Correction

Learned helplessness, and the paper its own author wrote against it. · published 6 September 2026

Watch on FacebookWatch on TikTokWatch on YouTube

One of the most influential ideas in psychology was published, taught for half a century, and then corrected by the people who discovered it — and the correction inverts what the phrase means.

The angle

Learned helplessness was taught for fifty years as the story of animals learning their actions were futile, until Maier and Seligman argued in 2016 that passivity is the brain’s default under prolonged aversive events and control is the thing that must be learned — which flips therapy from arguing someone out of a belief to giving them repeated experiences of agency.

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

The original work: Martin Seligman and Steven Maier, with Bruce Overmier, at the University of Pennsylvania in 1967. Dogs given electric shocks they could not escape later failed to escape shocks they could have escaped, simply by stepping over a low barrier. Animals that had experienced escapable shock did step over.

The interpretation that followed, and that is still taught: the animals had learned that their actions did not matter, and that learning generalised. The idea became one of the dominant models of depression, reformulated in 1978 by Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale to include how people explain events to themselves.

Fifty years later the two original authors published the reversal.

Maier and Seligman, “Learned Helplessness at Fifty: Insights from Neuroscience” (Psychological Review, 2016), argue the original account had it backwards. On the neuroscience, passivity and heightened anxiety are not learned at all: they are the default reaction to prolonged aversive events, produced by activity in the dorsal raphe nucleus. What an animal actually learns, when it has control, is that it has control — and detecting that control engages the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, which inhibits the default response and can be carried forward to later situations.

So the animals in 1967 did not learn to be helpless. They failed to learn that they were not. Helplessness was the starting point, and control is the thing that has to be acquired.

Seligman has been direct about this in interviews and in print: the theory as originally stated was wrong, even though the phenomenon it described was real.

What this changes

If helplessness is the default and control is what is learned, the therapeutic emphasis moves from talking someone out of a belief to giving them experiences of agency that build the inhibitory circuit.

What this does not show

The behavioural finding itself replicates and is not in dispute — animals subjected to uncontrollable aversive events do show the deficits described. The 2016 account is a reinterpretation supported by neuroscience, not a retraction, and it is a theory in its own right rather than a settled fact. It also should not be stretched into a claim about human depression being solved.

The original experiments would not pass an ethics board today, and that is worth saying plainly rather than leaving implied.
