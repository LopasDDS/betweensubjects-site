Eagle Eyesight Is About 2.4x Yours, Not 8x — Here’s Why

How much sharper an eagle’s eyesight actually is, measured. · published 1 October 2026

The notes this episode was written from

These are the working notes, unedited. Everything the video states as fact comes from here, and nothing else may be stated as fact. The sections on limits and on what the finding does not show are part of the brief, not an afterthought — see the method.

The popular claim. Eagles are routinely said to see four to eight times better than a human, sometimes more. The figure is repeated without a source attached to it, and the thing being multiplied is rarely specified - sharpness, distance, and field of view are different quantities.

The study. Lindsay Reymond, “Spatial visual acuity of the eagle Aquila audax: a behavioural, optical and anatomical investigation”, Vision Research, volume 25, issue 10, 1985. The subject was the wedge-tailed eagle, Australia’s largest raptor, and the work combined three independent approaches: a behavioural test, measurement of the eye’s optics, and anatomical counts of the retina.

What the behavioural test did. A small number of trained captive eagles were taught to discriminate a striped pattern from a plain grey field of the same average brightness, choosing the striped one for a food reward. The stripes were then made progressively finer until the bird could no longer tell them apart. The finest grating it could still reliably pick out gives its acuity in the same units used to measure human vision.

What it found. The wedge-tailed eagle’s acuity came out at roughly twice that of a human with good eyesight - the paper reports a figure of about 2.4 times under its conditions. That is a real and substantial advantage, and it is the highest acuity reliably measured in any animal. It is not four to eight times.

Why the eagle’s eye is better at all. The retina’s receptor cells are packed more densely than a human’s, and the eagle’s eye is large relative to its head, which produces a bigger image on that retina. Both effects push in the same direction, and the anatomical counts in the paper agree with what the behaving bird could do - three methods converging on one answer is the strength of the study.

Limits

This is one species. A small number of captive trained birds were tested, under laboratory lighting, on a laboratory task. Grating acuity is one well-defined thing vision does, not the whole of it: it says nothing about motion detection, ultraviolet sensitivity, depth judgement, or how well a bird picks a moving rabbit out of scrub from height, which is the thing people usually mean. An animal can be unremarkable on a grating chart and formidable in the air.

What this does not show

It is not a claim that eagle vision is overrated. Twice human acuity is an enormous margin in a biological system, and a bird that also flies at height sees detail no person could resolve from the same position. The narrow point is that the specific multiplier in circulation is larger than anything the measurement supports, and that the measured figure comes from a real experiment anyone can look up.
