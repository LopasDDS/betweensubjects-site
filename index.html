What the famous psychology studies actually found.

Short videos about psychology and behavioural science research — what the well-known studies actually found, and where the popular retelling departs from the record. The Stanford prison guards, Milgram’s fourth prod, the Dunning-Kruger chart that is not in the Dunning-Kruger paper.

Why the retelling drifts

A finding gets simpler every time it is repeated. The qualifier goes first, then the sample size, then the condition that made it work. What is left is a clean story that the paper does not support — and it is usually the version that gets taught.

These videos go back to what was actually measured. Often the real finding is stranger than the myth: Milgram ran more than twenty variations and the prod that sounded most like a direct order was the one nobody obeyed; Robbers Cave worked on the second attempt, after the first collapsed because the boys had been allowed to become friends first.

Just as often the correction cuts the other way. The Kitty Genovese story is mostly wrong and the bystander effect is real anyway. Saying so is the point — how each episode is checked.

Latest episodes

  • 18 detection dog teams searched empty rooms — 200+ alerts

    15 September 2026

    Eighteen trained detection dog teams searched rooms that contained no drugs and no explosives, and alerted over two hundred times — most often exactly where their handlers had been told, falsely, that something was hidden. The dogs were reading the humans, which is Clever Hans with a working animal and higher stakes.

  • He set out to prove apes build sentences. Then the tapes.

    14 September 2026

    Chimpanzees taught sign language really did learn hundreds of signs and use them to name and request — but the headline claim, that they were building sentences, collapsed when the researcher trying to prove it watched his own videotapes and found the apes were mostly echoing whatever their teacher had just signed.

  • Tolman’s rats: the latent learning story textbooks trim

    13 September 2026

    Rats that ran a maze for ten days with no reward looked like they had learned nothing, then performed almost perfectly the day after food appeared — which Tolman read as learning that had been happening invisibly all along. The textbook version has this defeating behaviourism; in fact rival explanations existed, the argument ended in a stalemate, and the experiment the phrase “cognitive map” comes from failed to replicate.

  • Alex the Parrot’s Last Words Weren’t a Goodbye

    12 September 2026

    Alex the grey parrot learned about a hundred labels and, under controls built specifically to rule out the Clever Hans effect, could answer questions about colour, shape and number — and the last words so often quoted as his farewell were what he said to Pepperberg every single night.

  • Skinner’s superstitious pigeons: what the 1971 rerun saw

    12 September 2026

    Eight pigeons fed on a timer regardless of what they did developed odd repeated movements, which Skinner explained as accidental reinforcement and compared to human superstition — but a careful replication found the behaviour converged across birds and tracked the interval, looking more like a food-anticipation sequence the species already had than a ritual built out of coincidence.

  • Alpha wolf: the biologist who spent 20 years taking it back

    11 September 2026

    The alpha wolf was described in unrelated captive wolves crammed into a zoo enclosure in 1947; wild packs turned out to be families led by the parents, with no rank to fight for, and the biologist whose book made the term famous argued against it for decades and could not get that book out of print until 2022.
