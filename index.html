What the famous studies actually found.

Short videos about psychology, behavioural science and animal cognition — what the well-known studies actually found, and where the popular retelling departs from the record. Milgram’s fourth prod, the Dunning-Kruger chart that is not in the Dunning-Kruger paper, the biologist who spent twenty years trying to unpublish “alpha wolf”.

Why the retelling drifts

A finding gets simpler every time it is repeated. The qualifier goes first, then the sample size, then the condition that made it work. What is left is a clean story that the paper does not support — and it is usually the version that gets taught.

These videos go back to what was actually measured. Often the real finding is stranger than the myth: Milgram ran more than twenty variations and the prod that sounded most like a direct order was the one nobody obeyed; Robbers Cave worked on the second attempt, after the first collapsed because the boys had been allowed to become friends first.

Just as often the correction cuts the other way. The Kitty Genovese story is mostly wrong and the bystander effect is real anyway. Saying so is the point — how each episode is checked.

  • Did you know? Seven of eight pigs passed. The repeat found two

    22 October 2026

    Seven of eight pigs given five hours with a mirror used the reflection to find food hidden behind a barrier, averaging 23 seconds, with smell and side-preference ruled out - but a later study with twenty-two piglets, published as “Lack of mirror use by pigs to locate food”, found only two animals doing it.

  • Did you know? Chilli makes a mole-rat nerve fire, and nothing happens

    21 October 2026

    Naked mole-rats show almost no pain behaviour to acid or capsaicin, but respond normally to other noxious stimuli and develop mechanical hypersensitivity after inflammation. For capsaicin the receptor is present and the nerves fire, and the signal fails later for want of substance P; for acid the nerves never fire at all - plausibly because their burrows are carbon-dioxide rich enough to make acid sensing a constant nuisance.

  • Did you know? Six cuttlefish waited two minutes for a better meal

    20 October 2026

    Six cuttlefish declined an immediately available piece of prawn in order to get a live grass shrimp they preferred, maintaining delays of roughly 50 to 130 seconds, and the individuals that waited longest were also the quickest to learn and re-learn a colour association - in an animal with none of the traits usually invoked to explain self-control.

  • Did you know? The first animal to pass the teaching test was an ant

    19 October 2026

    A Temnothorax ant leading a nestmate to a destination slows down for the follower, waits when contact is broken, and gets the follower there faster than it would have arrived alone - satisfying all three parts of the standard biological definition of teaching, the first non-human animal shown to do so, and prompting an argument about whether the definition is the right one.

  • Did you know? A bowerbird rebuilt its illusion in three days

    14 October 2026

    Male great bowerbirds place the smallest objects nearest the female’s viewing position and larger ones further away, creating forced perspective; when researchers reversed the size gradient the males restored it within about three days, and males producing the strongest illusions mated most - though why the illusion helps is unresolved.

  • Did you know? Alligators balance sticks in bird nesting season

    13 October 2026

    Mugger crocodiles and American alligators were observed resting with sticks balanced on their snouts, predominantly during the weeks when wading birds were gathering nest material, which is hard to explain as debris landing at random - though no bird was ever observed being caught this way, and most of the watching was done at captive sites.
