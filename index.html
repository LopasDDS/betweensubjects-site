What the famous studies actually found.

Short videos about psychology, behavioural science and animal cognition — what the well-known studies actually found, and where the popular retelling departs from the record. Milgram’s fourth prod, the Dunning-Kruger chart that is not in the Dunning-Kruger paper, the biologist who spent twenty years trying to unpublish “alpha wolf”.

Why the retelling drifts

A finding gets simpler every time it is repeated. The qualifier goes first, then the sample size, then the condition that made it work. What is left is a clean story that the paper does not support — and it is usually the version that gets taught.

These videos go back to what was actually measured. Often the real finding is stranger than the myth: Milgram ran more than twenty variations and the prod that sounded most like a direct order was the one nobody obeyed; Robbers Cave worked on the second attempt, after the first collapsed because the boys had been allowed to become friends first.

Just as often the correction cuts the other way. The Kitty Genovese story is mostly wrong and the bystander effect is real anyway. Saying so is the point — how each episode is checked.

  • Wolves Howl Most for Their Closest Packmate, Not Stress

    30 September 2026

    When a wolf was walked away from its pack, the wolves left behind howled in proportion to how close they were to that particular animal and how high its rank was, not in proportion to their measured stress hormones, which suggests howling is a socially directed signal rather than a pure distress reflex.

  • Your cat’s 65% secure attachment — one of two answers

    29 September 2026

    The study behind “science proves your cat is attached to you” found secure attachment in about 65 per cent of cats, the same share as human infants. A more tightly controlled study four years earlier found no secure attachment at all, and a 2025 paper argues the test was never the right tool for a solitary hunter. The number you saw is real. It is also one of two answers.

  • Toxoplasma’s ’fatal attraction’ looks cruder than reported

    28 September 2026

    A parasite really does change infected rodents’ behaviour in a way that helps it reach a cat - but the famous part, that it selectively deletes the fear of cats, did not survive the follow-up. It makes them generally less anxious, which is messier and less clever. And the version about humans, the one that made it into every headline, does not show up in the best study anyone has run.

  • Tsavo Lions: the death toll was likely 4x too high

    27 September 2026

    The Tsavo lions killed perhaps 35 people, not the 135 of the legend, and only one of them did most of it. They were not desperate: their teeth show soft feeding, not the bone-cracking of starvation. And the broken tooth that may have started it held the hair of everything they ate, which was sequenced in 2024.

  • The last wild horse may not be wild — 9 animals saved it

    26 September 2026

    The animal sold as the last truly wild horse may be the escaped livestock of a culture that vanished five thousand years ago - and specialists are still fighting about it. What did survive from the same work is that horse domestication did not start where everyone said it did. Meanwhile every Przewalski’s horse on earth descends from nine animals that made it through the 1940s.

  • Bees Flown 250m Off Course Still Flew the Dance’s Vector

    25 September 2026

    Von Frisch decoded the honeybee waggle dance and won a Nobel for it, while a rival spent decades arguing the bees were just following a smell. In 2005 researchers put radar transponders on recruits, displaced them 250 metres, and watched them fly the exact wrong-but-correct vector the dance had specified. Both men turned out to have half of it.
