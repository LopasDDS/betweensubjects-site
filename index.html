What the famous studies actually found.

Short videos about psychology, behavioural science and animal cognition — what the well-known studies actually found, and where the popular retelling departs from the record. Milgram’s fourth prod, the Dunning-Kruger chart that is not in the Dunning-Kruger paper, the biologist who spent twenty years trying to unpublish “alpha wolf”.

Why the retelling drifts

A finding gets simpler every time it is repeated. The qualifier goes first, then the sample size, then the condition that made it work. What is left is a clean story that the paper does not support — and it is usually the version that gets taught.

These videos go back to what was actually measured. Often the real finding is stranger than the myth: Milgram ran more than twenty variations and the prod that sounded most like a direct order was the one nobody obeyed; Robbers Cave worked on the second attempt, after the first collapsed because the boys had been allowed to become friends first.

Just as often the correction cuts the other way. The Kitty Genovese story is mostly wrong and the bystander effect is real anyway. Saying so is the point — how each episode is checked.

  • Bees Flown 250m Off Course Still Flew the Dance’s Vector

    25 September 2026

    Von Frisch decoded the honeybee waggle dance and won a Nobel for it, while a rival spent decades arguing the bees were just following a smell. In 2005 researchers put radar transponders on recruits, displaced them 250 metres, and watched them fly the exact wrong-but-correct vector the dance had specified. Both men turned out to have half of it.

  • Horses remember the angry photo — with the left eye

    24 September 2026

    Show a horse a photograph of an angry stranger, and hours later, when that stranger walks in with a neutral face, the horse looks at them with the eye it uses for threats - and only at them. The study behind the headline before it was picked apart for a real statistical error: proving a result in one condition and failing to prove it in another is not proof the two differ.

  • The lemming death march was staged: pushed off a riverbank

    23 September 2026

    The lemming death-march is a scene from a 1958 Disney documentary that won an Oscar, filmed hundreds of miles from the sea with animals bought and trucked in, herded on a turntable to look like a horde and then pushed off a riverbank. Real lemmings do disperse when crowded, and some drown crossing water. None of them line up to die.

  • Guide dogs carry the Labrador hunger gene 4x as often

    22 September 2026

    Labradors really do have a genetic reason to be hungry — a deletion in POMC that damages part of the signal telling them they are full, worth about 1.9 kg per copy. The twist is where it is concentrated: assistance dogs carry it at nearly four times the rate of pets, because people breeding for trainability were unknowingly selecting for appetite. Those dogs are not overweight. Same gene, different routine.

  • The Hawthorne data nobody looked at for eighty years

    21 September 2026

    The study that taught everyone people work harder when watched was, for eighty years, described by people who had not seen its data. When two economists finally found the original records, the famous pattern — output rising after every change, even the ones that made the room darker — was not in them. Something small survives. The story does not.

  • Betty the crow’s wire hook: maybe not invention after all

    21 September 2026

    A crow bending a wire into a hook was for years the best evidence an animal could invent a tool on the spot — until researchers went to New Caledonia, watched wild crows make their own tools, and found that ten of eighteen of them bend the shaft as a matter of course. Betty was wild-caught. She may have arrived already knowing the move, and the only genuinely new thing in the room was the wire.
