What the famous studies actually found.

Short videos about psychology, behavioural science and animal cognition — what the well-known studies actually found, and where the popular retelling departs from the record. Milgram’s fourth prod, the Dunning-Kruger chart that is not in the Dunning-Kruger paper, the biologist who spent twenty years trying to unpublish “alpha wolf”.

Why the retelling drifts

A finding gets simpler every time it is repeated. The qualifier goes first, then the sample size, then the condition that made it work. What is left is a clean story that the paper does not support — and it is usually the version that gets taught.

These videos go back to what was actually measured. Often the real finding is stranger than the myth: Milgram ran more than twenty variations and the prod that sounded most like a direct order was the one nobody obeyed; Robbers Cave worked on the second attempt, after the first collapsed because the boys had been allowed to become friends first.

Just as often the correction cuts the other way. The Kitty Genovese story is mostly wrong and the bystander effect is real anyway. Saying so is the point — how each episode is checked.

Latest episodes

  • Did you know? Dolphins answered their own whistle, voice stripped out

    8 October 2026

    Wild bottlenose dolphins off Scotland were played synthetic copies of their own signature whistles, stripped of voice characteristics so only the contour remained, and the group called that whistle back in 8 of 12 trials - significantly more than for whistles belonging to familiar or unfamiliar animals.

  • Eight Octopuses Learned to Tell Two Strangers Apart

    7 October 2026

    Eight giant Pacific octopuses at the Seattle Aquarium met two unfamiliar people over two weeks, one who always fed them and one who always prodded them with a bristly stick, and came to respond differently to the two - in body pattern, arm movement and funnel direction, with a breathing difference in the four largest animals only.

  • Sheep picked their handler out of a photo in ~72% of trials

    6 October 2026

    Eight sheep learned to pick particular human faces out of pairs of photographs, still managed it above chance when the faces were tilted to an unseen angle - dropping from roughly 79 to 67 per cent, a fall similar in size to the one people show - and chose their own handler’s photograph in about 72 per cent of trials without being trained to. A published response argued the sample and stimuli were too small to support the comparison with human ability.

  • Vampire bats feed who fed them, more than their kin

    5 October 2026

    Twenty vampire bats were individually fasted on 48 days across two years, and when they shared blood, the amount a bat had previously received from a given partner predicted what it gave back to that partner better than genetic relatedness did - about 8.5 times better, by the paper’s own comparison.

  • Wild Birds That Answer a Specific Human Call

    4 October 2026

    Greater honeyguides in northern Mozambique were played three sounds on real honey-hunting trips, and the traditional Yao “brrr-hm” call doubled the chance of being guided, from 33 to 66 per cent, and raised the chance of being shown a bees’ nest from 16 to 54 per cent, against arbitrary human words and another bird’s call - a wild, untrained animal responding to a specific signal from people.

  • Goldfish three-second memory: no study ever found it

    3 October 2026

    No study has ever found a three-second memory in goldfish; the number has no source. Eight goldfish given a lever that released food for only one hour a day learned to press it around that hour, and in a related experiment on a different species, rainbowfish that learned to escape a model trawl still escaped quickly when shown it eleven months later.
