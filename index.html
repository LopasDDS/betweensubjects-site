What the famous psychology studies actually found.

Short videos about psychology and behavioural science research — what the well-known studies actually found, and where the popular retelling departs from the record. The Stanford prison guards, Milgram’s fourth prod, the Dunning-Kruger chart that is not in the Dunning-Kruger paper.

Why the retelling drifts

A finding gets simpler every time it is repeated. The qualifier goes first, then the sample size, then the condition that made it work. What is left is a clean story that the paper does not support — and it is usually the version that gets taught.

These videos go back to what was actually measured. Often the real finding is stranger than the myth: Milgram ran more than twenty variations and the prod that sounded most like a direct order was the one nobody obeyed; Robbers Cave worked on the second attempt, after the first collapsed because the boys had been allowed to become friends first.

Just as often the correction cuts the other way. The Kitty Genovese story is mostly wrong and the bystander effect is real anyway. Saying so is the point — how each episode is checked.

  • Alpha wolf: the biologist who spent 20 years taking it back

    11 September 2026

    The alpha wolf was described in unrelated captive wolves crammed into a zoo enclosure in 1947; wild packs turned out to be families led by the parents, with no rank to fight for, and the biologist whose book made the term famous argued against it for decades and could not get that book out of print until 2022.

  • 13 experts found no trick. The horse still couldn’t count

    11 September 2026

    A horse that appeared to do arithmetic was reading involuntary changes in the questioner’s body and stopping when they relaxed — 89 per cent right when he could see the person asking, 6 per cent when he could not — and the investigation that worked this out is why double-blind procedure is standard.

  • 274 Social Science Claims Retested: Survivors Shrank

    11 September 2026

    When 274 published social science claims were retested with well-powered, pre-approved replications, about 55 per cent came out significant in the same direction and about half the papers held up — and the effects that did survive were, at the median, less than half the size first reported.

  • Robbers Cave failed once: the boys spotted the setup

    10 September 2026

    The most-cited demonstration that competition turns groups against each other worked on the second attempt. The first attempt failed because the boys already knew each other, saw what the adults were doing, and sided with each other against them — and the published design quietly made sure that could not happen again.

  • The broken glass that wasn’t there: how memory rebuilds

    9 September 2026

    Under the right conditions a person can be given a detailed, sincerely held memory of something that never happened — which says something about how memory is built, and much less than it is often used to say about whether to believe someone.

  • Power posing: the author who stopped believing her own study

    8 September 2026

    The most watched psychology talk in the world rests on a paper whose first author has since written, in public, that she does not believe the result — while the milder version of the claim, that it changes how you feel, is still standing.
